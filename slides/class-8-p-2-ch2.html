2.1 Doubling a Square

📐 Baudhāyana's Question: How to Double a Square?

Page 33–34
📜 The Śulba-Sūtra Verse 1.9 (c. 800 BCE)

In ancient ritual altar construction, Vedic mathematicians needed to double altar surface areas while keeping them strictly square.

  • Common Pitfall: Doubling the sidelength quadruples the area ($2 \times 2 = 4\times\text{ original area}$).
  • Baudhāyana's Discovery: "The diagonal of a square produces a square of double the area of the original square."
$$\text{Area of Square on Diagonal} = 2 \times \text{Area of Original Square}$$

2.2 Halving a Square

✂️ Halving a Square: Inscribing & Origami Folds

Page 36
🔄 Reversing the Construction

To construct a square having half the area of a given square:

  • Join the midpoints of the four outer sides ($P, Q, R, S$).
  • The newly created tilted square $PQRS$ has vertices resting on the midpoints of the original square.
  • Paper Folding Activity: Take a square sheet, fold the four outer corners inward toward the center. The four corner flaps fold flat without overlap, proving the area inside is exactly half ($\frac{1}{2}$).

2.3 Isosceles Triangle

📐 Hypotenuse of a Unit Isosceles Right Triangle

Page 37
🔍 The Unit Isosceles Right Triangle ($1, 1, c$)

Consider a right-angled triangle with perpendicular legs equal to $1\text{ unit}$.

  • Two such triangles form a unit square of area $1 \times 1 = 1\text{ sq. unit}$.
  • The square erected on the hypotenuse $c$ has double the area of this unit square: $$\text{Area of square on } c = 2 \times 1 = 2\text{ sq. units}$$
  • Since Area $= c^2$, we have: $$c^2 = 2 \implies c = \sqrt{2}$$
$$\text{Hypotenuse } c = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142...$$
🏷️ Key Vocabulary
  • Hypotenuse: In any right-angled triangle, the side opposite the $90^\circ$ right angle. It is always the longest side.
  • Radical $\sqrt{2}$: The positive real number whose square is strictly $2$.
  • Geometric Existence: Even before calculating its decimal expansion, $\sqrt{2}$ exists as a concrete, constructible physical length!

2.3 Nature of $\sqrt{2}$

🔢 Squeezing $\sqrt{2}$: Bounding the Value

Page 38
1. Integer Bounds

Compare areas of squares: $$1^2 = 1 < 2 < 4 = 2^2$$ $$\mathbf{1 < \sqrt{2} < 2}$$

Lower bound $= 1$, Upper bound $= 2$.

2. One Decimal Place

Testing tenths: $$1.4^2 = 1.96 < 2$$ $$1.5^2 = 2.25 > 2$$ $$\mathbf{1.4 < \sqrt{2} < 1.5}$$

3. Three Decimal Places

Testing thousandths: $$1.414^2 = 1.999396 < 2$$ $$1.415^2 = 2.002225 > 2$$ $$\mathbf{1.414 < \sqrt{2} < 1.415}$$

$$\text{Why can't } \sqrt{2} \text{ terminate? If } \sqrt{2} = 1.414\dots d \implies (\dots d)^2 \text{ has last digit } d^2 \neq 0 \text{ (never ending in } .000\dots)$$

2.3 Number Theory

🏛️ Euclid's Proof: Why $\sqrt{2} \neq \frac{m}{n}$

Page 39
📜 Proof by Prime Factor Parity (c. 300 BCE)

Can $\sqrt{2}$ be expressed as a common fraction $\frac{m}{n}$ of positive integers?

Assume $\sqrt{2} = \frac{m}{n} \implies 2 = \frac{m^2}{n^2} \implies \mathbf{2n^2 = m^2}$
  • In the prime factorisation of any perfect square ($m^2$ or $n^2$), the prime factor 2 must appear an even number of times.
  • Therefore, in $2n^2$, the factor 2 appears: $1 + \text{even} = \mathbf{\text{odd number of times}}$.
  • In $m^2$, the factor 2 appears: $\mathbf{\text{even number of times}}$.
  • Contradiction! An odd number cannot equal an even number.
♾️ A Non-Terminating Non-Repeating Decimal

Since $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be written as a terminating decimal or as any fraction $\frac{m}{n}$, its decimal expansion goes on forever without repeating:

$$\sqrt{2} = 1.414213562373095\dots$$

This marked humanity's first discovery of irrational numbers.

2.3 General Formula

📐 General Isosceles Right Triangle: $c^2 = 2a^2$

Page 40
🔺 Scaling the Equal Sides

Let an isosceles right triangle have equal perpendicular legs of length $a$ and hypotenuse $c$:

$$c^2 = 2a^2 \iff c = a\sqrt{2}$$
  • The area of the square on the diagonal is double the square on leg $a$.
  • Example 1 (Leg $a = 12$): $$c^2 = 2(12^2) = 288 \implies c = \sqrt{288}$$ Since $16^2 = 256$ and $17^2 = 289$, $c$ lies between $16$ and $17\text{ units}$.
🔍 Example 2: Finding Leg from Hypotenuse

If the hypotenuse $c = \sqrt{72}$, find the equal sidelengths $a$:

$$c^2 = 2a^2 \implies (\sqrt{72})^2 = 2a^2$$ $$72 = 2a^2 \implies a^2 = \frac{72}{2} = 36$$ $$\mathbf{a = \sqrt{36} = 6\text{ units}}$$

Both perpendicular legs have an exact integer length of 6 units.

2.4 Dissection Proof

📜 Baudhāyana's Geometric Dissection Proof

Page 42–44
✂️ Instructions from Śulba-Sūtra Verse 2.1
  • Join square of side $a$ adjacent to square of side $b$ ($b > a$).
  • Mark a strip of width $a$ along the right side of the larger square. Its base is divided into $(b-a)$ and $a$.
  • Draw the diagonal of this $a \times b$ rectangle to form a right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$, hypotenuse $c$.
  • Replicate this triangle 4 times ($T, U, W, X$) around the inner central region $V$.
  • The newly assembled figure $(T + U + V)$ is a perfect square of sidelength $c$!
⚖️ Area Conservation Proof

Notice that triangles $T, U, W, X$ are all congruent ($ \text{Area } = \frac{1}{2}ab$):

$$\text{Original Total Area} = \text{Area}(V) + \text{Area}(W) + \text{Area}(X)$$ $$\text{New Square Area} = \text{Area}(V) + \text{Area}(T) + \text{Area}(U)$$ Since $\text{Area}(T) + \text{Area}(U) = \text{Area}(W) + \text{Area}(X)$: $$\mathbf{c^2 = a^2 + b^2}$$

Fundamental Theorem

⭐ The Baudhāyana-Pythagoras Theorem

Page 46–47
$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$
📖 Formal Statement

If a right-angled triangle has shorter perpendicular sides of lengths $a$ and $b$, and hypotenuse of length $c$, then: $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$

🏛️ Historical Credit

Baudhāyana (c. 800 BCE): First person in recorded history to state this theorem in full generality.

Pythagoras (c. 500 BCE): Greek philosopher-mathematician who studied it two centuries later.

🎯 The 3–4–5 Triangle

If shorter legs are $3\text{ cm}$ and $4\text{ cm}$: $$3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$$ $$c = \sqrt{25} = \mathbf{5\text{ cm}}$$ Predicts exact measurements effortlessly!

2.5 Baudhāyana Triples

🔢 Right Triangles Having Integer Sidelengths

Page 48
📜 Ancient Vedic Triples (Verse 1.13)

Baudhāyana explicitly listed integer triples $(a, b, c)$ that satisfy $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$:

$$(3, 4, 5), \quad (5, 12, 13), \quad (8, 15, 17),$$ $$(7, 24, 25), \quad (12, 35, 37), \quad (15, 36, 39)$$

These are officially called Baudhāyana triples (or Pythagorean triples).

📈 Infinite Scaling Theorem

If $(a, b, c)$ is a Baudhāyana triple, then $(ka, kb, kc)$ is also a triple for any positive integer $k$:

$$(ka)^2 + (kb)^2 = k^2(a^2 + b^2) = k^2 c^2 = (kc)^2$$
  • For $(3, 4, 5) \implies (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), (12, 16, 20), \dots$
  • This proves there are infinitely many triples!

2.5 Generating Triples

⚡ Primitive Triples & The Odd Number Pattern

Page 49–50
🌟 Primitive Baudhāyana Triples

A triple is primitive if $\gcd(a, b, c) = 1$ (no common factor $> 1$).
Examples: $(3, 4, 5)$ and $(5, 12, 13)$ are primitive; $(6, 8, 10)$ is non-primitive.

$$\text{Sum of first } n \text{ odd numbers} = n^2$$

$$(n-1)^2 + (2n-1) = n^2$$ If the $n$-th odd number $(2n-1)$ is itself a perfect square $b^2$, we get an integer triple!

🔢 Generating Examples
  • Odd square $9$ ($n = 5$): $$(5-1)^2 + 9 = 5^2 \implies \mathbf{4^2 + 3^2 = 5^2} \implies (3, 4, 5)$$
  • Odd square $25$ ($n = 13$): $$(13-1)^2 + 25 = 13^2 \implies \mathbf{12^2 + 5^2 = 13^2} \implies (5, 12, 13)$$
  • Odd square $49$ ($n = 25$): $$(25-1)^2 + 49 = 25^2 \implies \mathbf{24^2 + 7^2 = 25^2} \implies (7, 24, 25)$$

2.6 Open Problems

📖 Fermat's Last Theorem: Beyond Powers of 2

Page 50–51
✍️ The Famous Margin Note (17th Century)

Pierre de Fermat studied Diophantus's *Arithmetica* and Baudhāyana triples $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$. He wondered: Can two cubes sum to a cube, or two fourth powers to a fourth power?

$$x^n + y^n = z^n \quad \text{has NO positive integer solutions for } n > 2$$

"I have found a truly marvellous proof of this statement, but the margin is too small to contain it."

🧒 Andrew Wiles' 30-Year Quest (1994)

For over 300 years, the greatest minds attempted and failed to find Fermat's proof.

  • In 1963, 10-year-old schoolboy Andrew Wiles read Eric Bell's *The Last Problem* and resolved to solve it.
  • After working 7 years in complete secrecy at Princeton, Wiles officially completed the proof in 1994 using elliptic curves and modular forms.

2.7 Historical Problem

🪷 Bhāskara II's Līlāvatī: The Lotus in the Lake

Page 52
📖 Verse from the 12th Century CE

"In a lake surrounded by chakra and krauñcha birds, a lotus flower peeps 1 unit above the water. Swayed by a gentle breeze, its tip touches the water 3 units away from its original stem. Quickly tell the depth of the lake!"

  • Let depth of lake $= x$
  • Total stem length $= x + 1$ (which acts as the hypotenuse when bent)
  • Horizontal drift $= 3\text{ units}$
⚡ Algebraic Solution
$$3^2 + x^2 = (x+1)^2$$
$$9 + x^2 = x^2 + 2x + 1$$ $$9 = 2x + 1 \implies 2x = 8 \implies \mathbf{x = 4\text{ units}}$$

Depth of the lake is exactly 4 units (Stem length $= 5$, forming a 3–4–5 triangle!).

2.7 Applications

💎 Applications: Rhombus Diagonals & Dot Grids

Page 53
1. Rhombus Sidelength

Diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other perpendicularly: $$d_1 = 24 \implies \text{half} = 12$$ $$d_2 = 70 \implies \text{half} = 35$$ $$\text{Side } s = \sqrt{12^2 + 35^2} = \sqrt{144 + 1225}$$ $$s = \sqrt{1369} = \mathbf{37\text{ units}}$$ (Forms Baudhāyana triple $(12, 35, 37)$).

2. Equilateral Altitude

In an equilateral triangle of side 6, altitude $h$ bisects the base into $3$ and $3$: $$h^2 + 3^2 = 6^2 \implies h^2 + 9 = 36$$ $$h = \sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt{3}$$ $$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 3\sqrt{3} = \mathbf{9\sqrt{3}\text{ sq units}}$$

3. Square Areas on a Grid

Square area equals $a^2 + b^2$ for grid vectors:

  • $1^2 + 1^2 = \mathbf{2\text{ sq units}}$ (diagonal of unit)
  • $2^2 + 0^2 = \mathbf{4\text{ sq units}}$
  • $2^2 + 1^2 = \mathbf{5\text{ sq units}}$
  • Impossible on grid: Area $3$, because $3$ cannot be written as $a^2 + b^2$ for integers!

Brain Teaser

🎲 It's Puzzle Time! Find the Colours!

Page 54
📦 The Three Mislabelled Boxes

There are 3 closed boxes:
• Box 1: Only RED balls
• Box 2: Only BLUE balls
• Box 3: Only GREEN balls

Crucial Condition: EVERY single box is labelled incorrectly (no box has its true label).
Challenge: You are allowed to draw one ball from only one box. How can you determine all three labels?
💡 The Logical Strategy
  • Pick the box labelled with the third color or inspect a specific box. Since all labels are wrong:
  • If you draw a ball from the box labelled "RED" and it is BLUE:
    That box is definitely the BLUE box.
  • The box labelled "GREEN" cannot be GREEN (because all labels are wrong), so it must be RED!
  • The remaining box labelled "BLUE" must be GREEN.
  • Pure mathematical deductive logic cracks the puzzle in 1 draw!

Exercise Highlights

⭐ Key Worked Exemplars: Exercises

Page 47, 53
1. Missing Leg Problem

Right triangle has hypotenuse $41\text{ cm}$ and one side $40\text{ cm}$. Find the third side $a$.

$$a^2 + 40^2 = 41^2$$ $$a^2 + 1600 = 1681$$ $$a^2 = 1681 - 1600 = 81$$ $$\mathbf{a = \sqrt{81} = 9\text{ cm}}$$ (Forms Baudhāyana triple $(9, 40, 41)$!)
2. Diagonal of a Square

Find the diagonal length of a square of side $5\text{ cm}$.

$$d^2 = 5^2 + 5^2 = 25 + 25 = 50$$ $$d = \sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \times 2} = \mathbf{5\sqrt{2}\text{ cm}}$$ $$\approx 5 \times 1.414 = \mathbf{7.07\text{ cm}}$$
3. Radical Sides Problem

Right triangle with legs $10$ and $\sqrt{150}$. Find hypotenuse $c$.

$$c^2 = 10^2 + (\sqrt{150})^2$$ $$c^2 = 100 + 150 = 250$$ $$\mathbf{c = \sqrt{250} = 5\sqrt{10}}$$ $$\approx \mathbf{15.81\text{ units}}$$

⭐ Advanced Worked Exemplars: Sidelengths & Bounds

Page 39, 47, 53
1. Decimal Sidelengths

Find hypotenuse $c$ when $a = 1.5$ and $b = 3.5$.

$$c^2 = (1.5)^2 + (3.5)^2$$ $$c^2 = 2.25 + 12.25 = 14.5$$ $$c = \sqrt{14.5} \approx \mathbf{3.81\text{ units}}$$ Bounds: $3.8 < \sqrt{14.5} < 3.9$ since $3.8^2 = 14.44$ and $3.9^2 = 15.21$.
2. Scaled Triple Verification

Given triangle with sides $27$, $x$, and hypotenuse $45$. Find $x$.

Observe common factor 9: $$27 = 3 \times 9, \quad 45 = 5 \times 9$$ Since $(3, 4, 5)$ is a triple: $$x = 4 \times 9 = \mathbf{36\text{ units}}$$ Check: $27^2 + 36^2 = 729 + 1296 = 2025 = 45^2$ ✅
3. Square Difference Area

Construct square of area $7^2 - 5^2$.

$$\text{Area} = 49 - 25 = 24\text{ sq units}$$ By Baudhāyana's theorem, this is the square on the leg of a right triangle with hypotenuse $7$ and other leg $5$: $$\text{Side} = \sqrt{24} \approx \mathbf{4.899\text{ units}}$$
