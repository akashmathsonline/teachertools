Thinking About 'Think of a Number'

🎲 The Classic Grade 7 Game

  • Step 1: Think of a number.
  • Step 2: Double it.
  • Step 3: Add four.
  • Step 4: Divide the total by two.
  • Step 5: Subtract the original number you thought of.
Prediction: The final answer is ALWAYS 2!

🔍 The Algebraic Reveal

Why does it work every single time regardless of what number is chosen?

  • Let the chosen starting number be $x$
  • Double it $\rightarrow 2x$
  • Add four $\rightarrow 2x + 4$
  • Divide by two $\rightarrow \dfrac{2x + 4}{2} = x + 2$
  • Subtract initial number $\rightarrow (x + 2) - x = 2$
Key Principle: The variable $x$ cancels out completely, leaving an invariant constant!

6.2 • BIRTHDAY / DATE TRICK Pages 136–137

Decoding Shubham & Mukta's Date Trick

📅 The Game Flow (Republic Day)

  • Mukta thinks of: 26/01 (Month $M=1$, Day $D=26$)
  • Multiply month by $5 \rightarrow 1 \times 5 = 5$
  • Add $6 \rightarrow 5 + 6 = 11$
  • Multiply by $4 \rightarrow 11 \times 4 = 44$
  • Add $9 \rightarrow 44 + 9 = 53$
  • Multiply by $5 \rightarrow 53 \times 5 = 265$
  • Add the day $D \rightarrow 265 + 26 = 291$

🧮 Algebraic Derivation

Let Month $= M$ and Day $= D$:

  • Multiply $M$ by $5 \rightarrow 5M$
  • Add $6 \rightarrow 5M + 6$
  • Multiply by $4 \rightarrow 4(5M + 6) = 20M + 24$
  • Add $9 \rightarrow 20M + 33$
  • Multiply by $5 \rightarrow 5(20M + 33) = 100M + 165$
  • Add day $D \rightarrow \mathbf{100M + 165 + D}$
$$\text{Final Result} - 165 = 100M + D$$ Since $D \le 31$, the last two digits represent $D$, and the leading digits represent $M$!

Number Pyramids: Rules and Structure

🧱 The Fundamental Rule

In a number pyramid, each number is the exact sum of the two numbers directly below it.

23
10
13
1
9
4

Notice: $1+9 = 10$, $9+4 = 13$, and $10+13 = 23$.

🔄 Bottom-Up vs. Working Backwards

  • Forward Addition: If bottom blocks are given, simply sum adjacent pairs upward.
  • Backward Subtraction: If higher blocks and partial lower blocks are known, subtract: $$\text{Missing Base Block} = \text{Top Block} - \text{Known Base Block}$$
  • Puzzles with Gaps: When gaps exist in both the middle and base, basic arithmetic alone fails and algebra becomes essential!

⚖️ Solving the Linear System

  • From middle row: $a = 12 + c$ and $b = c + 8$
  • Top block condition: $a + b = 60$
  • Substitute $a$ and $b$: $$(12 + c) + (c + 8) = 60$$

  • Back-substitute: $a = 12 + 20 = \mathbf{32}$, and $b = 20 + 8 = \mathbf{28}$
Letter-numbers bridge the missing link between separated cells!

6.3 • GENERAL PYRAMID FORMULAS Pages 139–140

General Formulas for Pyramid Peaks

🔺 3-Row Pyramid (Base: $a, b, c$)

Tracking the propagation of variables up the pyramid:

$$\text{Top} = a + 2b + c$$
  • Row 1 (Bottom): $a, \quad b, \quad c$
  • Row 2 (Middle): $(a+b), \quad (b+c)$
  • Row 3 (Top): $(a+b) + (b+c) = a + 2b + c$
Coefficients are 1, 2, 1 (Row 2 of Pascal's Triangle)!

🔺 4-Row Pyramid (Base: $a, b, c, d$)

Continuing the sum upward through four levels:

$$\text{Top} = a + 3b + 3c + d$$
  • Row 2: $(a+b), \quad (b+c), \quad (c+d)$
  • Row 3: $(a+2b+c), \quad (b+2c+d)$
  • Row 4 (Top): $a + 3b + 3c + d$
Coefficients are 1, 3, 3, 1 (Row 3 of Pascal's Triangle)!

6.3 • MATHEMATICAL CONNECTIONS Page 140

Virahāṅka–Fibonacci Numbers in Pyramids

📜 Historical Sequence

Named after the ancient Indian grammarian Virahāṅka (~600 CE):

$$F_1=1, \; F_2=2, \; F_3=3, \; F_4=5, \; F_5=8, \; F_6=13, \dots$$

Each term is the sum of the preceding two: $F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}$.

Special Property: If consecutive Virahāṅka–Fibonacci numbers are placed at the base, what happens to every cell in the pyramid?

🔍 3-Row and n-Row Behavior

  • 3-Row with $1, 2, 3$:
    Row 2 has $1+2 = 3$ and $2+3 = 5$.
    Row 3 (Top) has $3+5 = 8$.
    Grid entries: $1, 2, 3, 5, 8$ — ALL are Virahāṅka numbers!
  • General $n$-Row Pyramid:
    Because adjacent base cells sum directly into the next Fibonacci number, the entire pyramid generates successive shifts of the sequence!

6.5 • OPTIMIZATION PUZZLE Pages 142–144

The Largest Product: $[\;][\;] \times [\;]$

🔢 Case Study: Digits 2, 3, 5

There are 6 possible ways to form a 2-digit by 1-digit product:

  • Multiplier 2: $35 \times 2 = 70$ vs $53 \times 2 = 106$
  • Multiplier 3: $25 \times 3 = 75$ vs $52 \times 3 = 156$
  • Multiplier 5: $23 \times 5 = 115$ vs $32 \times 5 = 160$
Comparing: $52 \times 3 = 156$  vs  $32 \times 5 = 160$

Winner: $32 \times 5 = 160$ is the largest possible product!

📐 General Algebraic Proof

Let three digits be $p < q < r$. To maximize, compare the two top candidates:

  • Candidate A ($r$ as multiplier): $$qp \times r = (10q + p)r = 10qr + pr$$
  • Candidate B ($q$ as multiplier): $$rp \times q = (10r + p)q = 10rq + pq$$
Comparing second terms: Since $r > q$, we have $pr > pq$.
Therefore, $qp \times r > rp \times q$ ALWAYS!

Rule: Place the largest digit as the single multiplier, and arrange the remaining digits in descending order!

6.6 • DIVISIBILITY TRICKS Pages 144–145

Decoding Digit Reversal & Divisibility

🔄 Difference of Reversed 2-Digit Numbers

  • Start with any 2-digit number: $ab = 10a + b$
  • Reverse the digits: $ba = 10b + a$
  • Assume $b > a$, then the difference is: $$(10b + a) - (10a + b) = 9b - 9a = \mathbf{9(b - a)}$$
  • Result: Always divisible by 9 with remainder 0!
  • Quotient: The quotient is simply the difference of digits $(b - a)$!

➕ Sum of Reversed 2-Digit Numbers

What if we ADD the original number and its reverse?

$$(10a + b) + (10b + a) = 11a + 11b = \mathbf{11(a + b)}$$
  • $31 + 13 = 44 = 11 \times 4$
  • $28 + 82 = 110 = 11 \times 10$
  • $12 + 21 = 33 = 11 \times 3$
Theorem: The sum of any 2-digit number and its reversal is ALWAYS divisible by 11!

6.6 • ADVANCED DIVISIBILITY Page 145

Cyclic Sums and the 1001 Miracle

🔄 Cyclic Permutations of $abc$

Take any 3-digit number $abc$ and form cycles $bca$ and $cab$:

  • $abc = 100a + 10b + c$
  • $bca = 100b + 10c + a$
  • $cab = 100c + 10a + b$
  • $\text{Sum} = 111a + 111b + 111c = \mathbf{111(a + b + c)}$
Since $111 = 3 \times 37$, the sum is ALWAYS divisible by both 3 and 37!

✨ The Repeated 6-Digit Number $abcabc$

Write a 3-digit number twice: e.g., $456456$

  • $abcabc = 1000 \times abc + abc = \mathbf{1001 \times abc}$
  • Notice the prime factorization of 1001: $$1001 = 7 \times 11 \times 13$$
  • Successively dividing by 7, 11, and 13 returns the original number $abc$ with zero remainder!

EXERCISE HIGHLIGHTS Pages 146–147

Representative Worked Solutions

🐴 Horses & Hens (Q6)

Given: 55 heads, 150 legs total.

Let horses $= H$, hens $= C$.
$H + C = 55 \implies C = 55 - H$
$4H + 2C = 150$
$4H + 2(55 - H) = 150$
$2H + 110 = 150 \implies 2H = 40$
Ans: $H = 20$ horses, $C = 35$ hens!

👩‍👧 Age Puzzle (Q7)

Given: Mother is $5\times$ daughter; in 6 yrs, $3\times$.

Let daughter's age now $= x$.
Mother's age now $= 5x$.
In 6 years:
$5x + 6 = 3(x + 6)$
$5x + 6 = 3x + 18$
$2x = 12 \implies x = 6$
Ans: Daughter is 6 years old now!

🧞 Karim & Genie (Q11)

Given: Coins double each round; pays 8 coins. Left with 0 after round 3.

Work backwards from Round 3:
End: $0 + 8 = 8$ coins $\implies$ started R3 with $8 / 2 = 4$.
R2: $4 + 8 = 12 \implies$ started R2 with $12 / 2 = 6$.
R1: $6 + 8 = 14 \implies$ started R1 with $14 / 2 = 7$.
Ans: Karim initially had 7 coins!

6.6 • PATTERN PROOFS Page 146

Sum of Consecutive Odds and Fractions (Q10)

🔍 The Surprising Sequence

Evaluate the sequence of grouped odd numbers:

  • $\dfrac{1}{3} = \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{3}}$
  • $\dfrac{1+3}{5+7} = \dfrac{4}{12} = \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{3}}$
  • $\dfrac{1+3+5}{7+9+11} = \dfrac{9}{27} = \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{3}}$
Every single fraction simplifies to $\dfrac{1}{3}$!

🧮 Algebraic Proof for $n$ Terms

  • Numerator: Sum of first $n$ odd numbers: $$1 + 3 + \dots + (2n - 1) = n^2$$
  • Denominator: Sum of the next $n$ odd numbers: $$\text{Sum}(2n \text{ odds}) - \text{Sum}(n \text{ odds}) = (2n)^2 - n^2 = 4n^2 - n^2 = 3n^2$$
  • The Fraction: $$\frac{\text{Numerator}}{\text{Denominator}} = \frac{n^2}{3n^2} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3}}$$
