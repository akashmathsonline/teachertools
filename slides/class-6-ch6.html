Section 6.1 Perimeter: Boundary of Plane Figures
Pages 129–130
📏 Definition of Perimeter

The perimeter of any closed plane figure is the continuous distance covered along its boundary when you go around it once completely.

$$\text{Perimeter of a Polygon} = \text{Sum of the lengths of all its sides}$$
Rectangle Perimeter Formula

Since opposite sides of a rectangle are equal ($AB = CD$ and $BC = DA$):

$$\text{Perimeter} = 2 \times (\text{Length} + \text{Breadth}) = 2(l + b)$$

Example: For $l = 12\text{ cm}, b = 8\text{ cm} \implies P = 2 \times (12 + 8) = 40\text{ cm}$.

Square Perimeter Formula

A square has 4 sides of identical length $s$:

$$\text{Perimeter of Square} = 4 \times \text{Length of a side} = 4s$$

Example: Putting colored tape around a photo frame of side $1\text{ m} \implies P = 4 \times 1 = 4\text{ m}$.

Section 6.1 Missing Dimensions & Invariant Perimeter
Pages 131–132
🔍 Working Backwards from Perimeter
  • Rectangle: Given $P$ and $b$, $\text{Length} = \frac{P}{2} - b$.
    Example: $P = 14\text{ cm}, b = 2\text{ cm} \implies l = 7 - 2 = 5\text{ cm}$.
  • Square: Side $= \frac{P}{4}$.
    Example: $P = 20\text{ cm} \implies \text{Side} = 5\text{ cm}$.
  • Triangle: Third side $= P - (s_1 + s_2)$.
    Example: $P = 55\text{ cm}, s_1 = 20, s_2 = 14 \implies s_3 = 55 - 34 = 21\text{ cm}$.
🔄 Wire Reshaping Principle

When a wire of fixed length is bent into different shapes, its perimeter remains constant!

$$\text{Wire of Rectangle } (5\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm}) \implies P = 2(5 + 3) = 16\text{ cm}$$ $$\text{Forming Square} \implies 4s = 16\text{ cm} \implies s = 4\text{ cm}$$

Fencing Cost: Cost $= \text{Perimeter} \times \text{Rate per metre}$. For multiple rounds, multiply total perimeter by number of rounds!

🏃 Outer vs. Inner Track Comparison

• Akshi (Outer Track $70\text{ m} \times 40\text{ m}$):
1 Round $= 2 \times (70 + 40) = 220\text{ m}$.
5 Rounds $= 5 \times 220\text{ m} = \mathbf{1100\text{ m}}$.

• Toshi (Inner Track $60\text{ m} \times 30\text{ m}$):
1 Round $= 2 \times (60 + 30) = 180\text{ m}$.
7 Rounds $= 7 \times 180\text{ m} = \mathbf{1260\text{ m}}$.

Toshi ran a longer distance ($1260\text{ m} > 1100\text{ m}$) despite the smaller track!
🏁 Deep Dive: Staggered Starts for Equal Races

For a $350\text{ m}$ race with a common finishing line on square tracks:

  • Inner Track ($100\text{ m}$ side): $P = 400\text{ m}$. To finish at the halfway mark, the start $A$ is placed $50\text{ m}$ before the corner!
  • Outer Track ($150\text{ m}$ side): $P = 600\text{ m}$. To run $350\text{ m}$ to the flag, start $B$ is placed on the opposite top side!

Section 6.1 Dot Grid Perimeters & Regular Polygons
Pages 134–136
📐 Straight vs. Diagonal Grid Units

On a square dot grid, lines connecting adjacent dots horizontally/vertically are straight units ($s$). Lines connecting diagonal dots are diagonal units ($d$).

$$\text{Diagonal Unit } (d) > \text{Straight Unit } (s) \quad [d = \sqrt{2}s \approx 1.414s]$$

Therefore, a triangle boundary of 6 straight segments and 3 diagonals has perimeter $6s + 3d > 9$ units!

⭐ General Regular Polygon Law

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal:

$$\text{Perimeter of Regular Polygon} = n \times \text{Length of one side}$$
  • Equilateral Triangle ($n = 3$): $P = 3 \times \text{side}$.
  • Square ($n = 4$): $P = 4 \times \text{side}$.
  • Regular Pentagon ($n = 5$): $P = 5 \times \text{side}$.
  • Regular Hexagon ($n = 6$): $P = 6 \times \text{side}$.

Section 6.1 Split and Rejoin: Perimeter Variations
Page 136
✂️ Cutting and Rearranging a $6\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm}$ Chit

Cut a $6\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm}$ rectangle into two identical $6\text{ cm} \times 2\text{ cm}$ strips. Both pieces have area $12\text{ cm}^2$ each (total $24\text{ cm}^2$).

Arrangement A (End-to-End)

Joined at $2\text{ cm}$ ends: creates a long $12\text{ cm} \times 2\text{ cm}$ strip.
Perimeter: $2(12 + 2) = \mathbf{28\text{ cm}}$.

Arrangement B (L-Shape)

Two strips overlap at a $2\text{ cm} \times 2\text{ cm}$ square corner.
Perimeter: Sum of exterior sides $= \mathbf{28\text{ cm}}$.

Original (Side-by-Side)

Joined along the full $6\text{ cm}$ edge: forms the original $6 \times 4$ rectangle.
Perimeter: $2(6 + 4) = \mathbf{20\text{ cm}}$.

✨ Core Discovery: Cutting and rearranging pieces changes the perimeter substantially even though the enclosed area remains completely identical!

Section 6.2 Area: Measure of Enclosed Surface
Pages 137–138
📐 Definition of Area

The amount of flat two-dimensional surface enclosed inside the boundary of a closed figure is called its area. Area is always measured in square units ($\text{sq cm}, \text{sq m}, \text{sq units}$).

Area of a Rectangle
$$\text{Area} = \text{Length} \times \text{Width} = l \times b$$

Represents the total number of unit squares filling $l$ columns and $b$ rows!

Area of a Square
$$\text{Area} = \text{Side} \times \text{Side} = s^2$$

Special case of rectangle where length equals width ($l = b = s$).

Real-World Subtraction of Areas

Uncarpeted Floor: Room $= 5\text{ m} \times 4\text{ m} = 20\text{ m}^2$. Square carpet $= 3\text{ m} \times 3\text{ m} = 9\text{ m}^2$.
$\text{Remaining Uncarpeted Area} = 20 - 9 = \mathbf{11\text{ m}^2}$.

Section 6.2 Tangram Puzzle: Fractional Area Ratios
Page 139
🧩 7-Piece Tangram Composition

The classic Chinese tangram consists of 7 pieces cut from one large square:

  • 2 Large Triangles ($A, B$)
  • 1 Medium Triangle ($F$)
  • 2 Small Triangles ($C, E$)
  • 1 Square ($D$)
  • 1 Parallelogram ($G$)
⚖️ Exact Multiples in Terms of Small Triangle C
• $\text{Area}(E) = \text{Area}(C) = 1 \text{ unit}$
• $\text{Area}(D) = \text{Area}(F) = \text{Area}(G) = 2 \times \text{Area}(C)$
• $\text{Area}(A) = \text{Area}(B) = 4 \times \text{Area}(C)$
• $\text{Total Big Square Area} = 16 \times \text{Area}(C)$

Area of any shape formed using all 7 pieces is strictly identical ($16 \times C$)!

Section 6.2 Estimating Irregular Areas & Circle Packing
Pages 140–141
📝 4-Step Grid Estimation Convention
  1. Count each fully enclosed square as $1\text{ sq unit}$.
  2. Ignore parts covering less than half a square.
  3. Count parts covering more than half as $1\text{ sq unit}$.
  4. Count parts covering exactly half as $\frac{1}{2}\text{ sq unit}$.

Enables measuring irregular leaves, lakes, and organic shapes with great precision!

⚪ Why Measure Area in Squares, Not Circles?

• The Gap Problem: Circles cannot tessellate (tile) a plane without leaving empty gaps.

• In a rectangle packed with circles, alignment variations yield different counts ($42$ vs $44$ circles)!

• Squares Tessellate Perfectly: Zero overlaps, zero gaps, simple base $\times$ height multiplication!

Section 6.3 Area of a Triangle: Half of a Rectangle
Pages 142–144
✂️ The Diagonal Bisection Principle

Cutting any rectangle along its diagonal produces two right triangles that overlap identically (congruent):

$$\text{Area of Right Triangle } \triangle BAD = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Area of Rectangle } ABCD$$

Both triangles have equal area, even though one is rotated relative to the other!

🔺 Any Triangle Inside a Rectangle

For any general triangle $\triangle ABE$ with apex $E$ on the top edge:

$$\text{Area}(\triangle AEF) = \frac{1}{2}\text{Area}(\text{Rectangle } AFED)$$ $$\text{Area}(\triangle BEF) = \frac{1}{2}\text{Area}(\text{Rectangle } BFEC)$$ $$\text{Area}(\triangle ABE) = \frac{1}{2}\text{Area}(\text{Rectangle } ABCD)$$

Universal Law: $\text{Area of Triangle} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base} \times \text{Height}$!

Section 6.3 Making it 'More' or 'Less': 9-Square Polyominoes
Page 145
🧱 Fixed Area ($9\text{ sq units}$) with Variable Perimeters

Using 9 unit squares connected edge-to-edge without holes, we can generate drastically different perimeters!

Smallest Perimeter: $12\text{ units}$

Arranged as a compact $3 \times 3$ square:
$P = 4 \times 3 = \mathbf{12\text{ units}}$.
(Maximal sharing of internal edges!)

Intermediate: $18\text{ units}$

Staircase or stepped cross arrangement with 9 squares gives $P = \mathbf{18\text{ units}}$. Multiple distinct shapes exist for $P = 18$!

Largest Perimeter: $20\text{ units}$

Arranged as a single straight $9 \times 1$ line or C-bracket:
$P = 2(9 + 1) = \mathbf{20\text{ units}}$.
(Minimal sharing of internal edges!)

📐 Isoperimetric Principle: For a given area, the more compact and square-like a figure is, the smaller its perimeter! The more stretched out or branched, the larger its perimeter!

Section 6.3 Area Maze Puzzles: Logical Deductions
Page 148
🧩 Puzzle A: 4-Quadrant Rectangle

• Top-left: $13\text{ cm}^2$, Top-right: $26\text{ cm}^2$
• Width ratio $= 26 / 13 = 2:1$.
• Bottom-left: $15\text{ cm}^2$.
• Since width doubles with same height:

$$\text{Missing Area} = 15 \times 2 = \mathbf{30\text{ cm}^2}$$
🧩 Puzzle B: Stepped Blocks

• Middle block: Area $10\text{ cm}^2$, width $= 2\text{ cm} \implies \text{height} = 5\text{ cm}$.
• Top block has width $3\text{ cm}$, height $= 5 - 2 = 3\text{ cm}$.

$$\text{Missing Area} = 3 \times 3 = \mathbf{9\text{ cm}^2}$$
🧩 Puzzle C: Cascading Heights

• Middle block: $42\text{ cm}^2$, height $6\text{ cm} \implies \text{width} = 7\text{ cm}$.
• Top block width $= 7 - 3 = 4\text{ cm}$, height $= 15 - (6 + 5) = 4\text{ cm}$.
Missing Area: $4 \times 4 = \mathbf{16\text{ cm}^2}$.

🧩 Puzzle D: Stepped Side Dimension

• Bottom block: $18\text{ cm}^2$, width $5\text{ cm} \implies \text{height} = 3.6\text{ cm}$ or integer stepped heights.
• Deducing shared heights: Missing Side $= \mathbf{5\text{ cm}}$.

Worked Examples Exercise Highlights & Solutions
Pages 148–149
Problem 1: Combined Area

Question: Find dimensions of a rectangle whose area equals the sum of $5\text{ m} \times 10\text{ m}$ and $2\text{ m} \times 7\text{ m}$.

$\text{Total Area} = 50 + 14 = 64\text{ m}^2$.
Factors: $16 \times 4, 32 \times 2, 8 \times 8$.

Answer: $16\text{ m} \times 4\text{ m}$ or $8\text{ m} \times 8\text{ m}$

Problem 2: Corner Beds

Question: Four flower beds $2\text{ m} \times 1\text{ m}$ dug at corners of garden $15\text{ m} \times 12\text{ m}$. Remaining lawn area?

$\text{Garden} = 15 \times 12 = 180\text{ m}^2$.
$\text{4 Beds} = 4 \times (2 \times 1) = 8\text{ m}^2$.
$\text{Lawn} = 180 - 8 = 172\text{ m}^2$.

Answer: $172\text{ sq m}$

Problem 3: Halving a Square

Question: A square of side $s$ is cut into two rectangles. What is true about their combined perimeter?

$\text{Square } P = 4s$.
Two rectangles $s \times \frac{s}{2}$:
$P_1 + P_2 = 2 \times 3s = 6s = 1.5(4s)$.

Answer: Statement (c) is true: $1\frac{1}{2}$ times!

Chapter Summary Core Takeaways & Formula Checklist
Page 150
✅ Perimeter Formulas Checklist
  • Polygon: Sum of the lengths of all outer boundary sides.
  • Rectangle: $P = 2 \times (\text{length} + \text{breadth}) = 2(l + b)$.
  • Square: $P = 4 \times \text{length of a side} = 4s$.
  • Regular Polygon: $P = n \times \text{side}$ ($n$ is the number of sides).
✅ Area Formulas & Properties Checklist
  • Rectangle Area: $\text{Area} = \text{length} \times \text{breadth} = l \times b$.
  • Square Area: $\text{Area} = \text{side} \times \text{side} = s^2$.
  • Triangle Area: $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$.
  • Independence: Figures with identical areas can have different perimeters; figures with identical perimeters can enclose different areas!
