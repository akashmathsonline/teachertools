Statistical Questions & Everyday Statements

Distinguishing deterministic facts from data-driven statistical inquiries
🗣️ Statistical Thinking in Daily Life
  • The Height Riddle: A teacher meets two friends: one is 5 ft tall, the other 6 ft tall. We guess the 5-ft person is a woman and 6-ft is a man because statistically, adult men are taller than women on average.
  • Statistical Statements: Claims, summaries, or predictions based on numbers, proportions, or variations:
    • "I take about 15 minutes to cycle from school to home."
    • "Jemimah's batting has been very consistent over the past year."
❓ What is a Statistical Question?
  • Definition: A question that can be answered by collecting data, where the data is expected to show variability.
  • Statistical:
    • "How tall are Grade 7 students in our school?" (Variability exists!)
    • "Typically, are onions costlier in Yahapur or Wahapur?"
  • Non-Statistical (Specific Fact):
    • "Do you like reading?" (Single personal opinion, not a data distribution)
💡 Statistics: The science of collecting, organising, analysing, interpreting, and presenting data to make sense of the world.

  • Yashasvi: $55, 67, 18, 35 \implies \text{Total} = 175\text{ runs}$.
  • Four Competing Perspectives:

⚖️ Series 2: The Unequal Matches Trap
  • Shubman (5 matches): $23, 7, 10, 52, 18 \implies \text{Total} = 110$.
  • Yashasvi (4 matches): $26, 53, 2, 15 \implies \text{Total} = 96$.
  • Total alone fails when group sizes differ!
  • Balancing Highs and Lows (Average): $$\text{Shubman's Average} = \frac{110}{5} = \mathbf{22\text{ runs/match}}$$ $$\text{Yashasvi's Average} = \frac{96}{4} = \mathbf{24\text{ runs/match}}$$
  • Yashasvi's average is higher despite having a lower total!
$$\text{Arithmetic Mean (Average)} = \frac{\text{Sum of all values}}{\text{Total number of values}}$$

Average as Fair-Share & Ancient Indian Heritage

Equalising uneven physical collections and classical Sanskrit terminology for the Mean
🍈 The Guava Harvesting Problem (Page 100)
  • Shreyas's Group (5 friends): Collected $3, 8, 10, 5, 4$ guavas.
    Total $= 30 \implies \text{Fair-Share} = \frac{30}{5} = \mathbf{6\text{ guavas each}}$.
  • Parag's Group (6 friends): Collected $5, 4, 6, 3, 4, 8$ guavas.
    Total $= 30 \implies \text{Fair-Share} = \frac{30}{6} = \mathbf{5\text{ guavas each}}$.
  • Visual Levelling: Rearranging columns of guavas until all stacks have the identical height equals finding the mean!
  • Vaishnavi's Hibiscus blooms: $2, 7, 9, 4, 3 \implies \frac{25}{5} = \mathbf{5\text{ flowers/day}}$.
📜 Ancient Indian Terms for Arithmetic Mean
  • Classical Indian mathematicians saw the mean as an "equaliser" (*Sama* = equal):
  • • Brahmagupta (628 CE): *Samarajju* (mean measure of a line segment).
  • • Mahāvīrācārya (850 CE): *Samīkaraṇa* (levelling, equalising).
  • • Śrīpati (1039 CE): *Sāmya* (equality, impartiality).
  • • Bhāskarācārya (1150 CE) & Gaṇeśa (1545 CE): *Samamiti* (mean measure).
🌟 Conceptual Core: The Mean is the exact value that each member would receive if the entire total was redistributed equally.

Visualising Variability: The Power of Dot Plots

Representing frequencies along a scaled axis to reveal clustering, spread, and gaps
📈 Anatomy of a Dot Plot
  • Horizontal Axis: Uniformly spaced numerical scale showing possible data values (e.g., ₹10 to ₹60).
  • Vertical Stacking: Each data occurrence is plotted as a single dot above that number. Multiple occurrences stack neatly upward!
  • Sorted by Nature: Dot plots automatically order the values from lowest to highest.
  • Shows clusters (dense areas), gaps (empty intervals), and spread at a single glance.
🔍 Comparing the Dot Plots of Onion Prices
  • Yahapur (Green):
    • Compact distribution between $24$ and $59$.
    • Dense cluster in the 20s and 30s. Zero values below $24$.
  • Wahapur (Purple):
    • Much more widely dispersed across the entire line ($17$ to $60$).
    • Has 2 low values between 11–20 ($17, 19$).
    • Stack of 2 dots at ₹$42$ indicates duplicate values.
  • Trade-off: The dot plot reveals distribution instantly, but loses the specific calendar month sequence.
✨ Key Insight: Dot plots help us see where the data accumulates, how wide the boundaries stretch, and whether values are tightly packed or scattered.

SECTION 5.2 OUTLIERS & MEDIAN
PAGE 105–106

When the Mean Fails: Outliers and the Median

How extreme values distort the arithmetic mean, and how the median restores balance
📏 The Family Height Paradox
  • Yaangba's Family (6 people):
    Heights: $155, 160, 164, 165, 169, 173\text{ cm}$.
    $\text{Mean} = \frac{986}{6} = \mathbf{164.3\text{ cm}}$. Balanced data!
  • Poovizhi's Family (5 people):
    Heights: $118, 165, 170, 173, 175\text{ cm}$.
    $\text{Mean} = \frac{801}{5} = \mathbf{160.2\text{ cm}}$.
  • The Distortion: Almost all members of Poovizhi's family are taller than Yaangba's, yet their mean is lower! Why?
  • Because the $118\text{ cm}$ child is an outlier that drags down the mean!
🎯 The Robust Solution: The Median
  • Definition: The exact middle value of data sorted in ascending order.
  • Odd Count (Poovizhi):
    Sorted: $118, 165, \mathbf{170}, 173, 175$.
    $\mathbf{\text{Median} = 170\text{ cm}}$ (Unshaken by the $118\text{ cm}$ outlier!).
  • Even Count (Yaangba):
    Sorted: $155, 160, \mathbf{164, 165}, 169, 173$.
    Take average of the 2 middle numbers: $$\text{Median} = \frac{164 + 165}{2} = \mathbf{164.5\text{ cm}}$$
⚠️ Outlier: An extreme value that significantly deviates from the rest of the data. The Mean is heavily sensitive to outliers; the Median is resistant and robust!

SECTION 5.2 OUTLIER SHIFTS
PAGE 107–108

Mean vs Median Shifts: High and Low Outliers

Analyzing how directional outliers pull the mean away from the median
📚 "Are You a Bookworm?" (High Outlier)
  • Stories read by 15 students: $$6, 8, 30, 2, 5, 15, 7, 12, 40, 0, 8, 10, 5, 1, 6$$
  • Sorted: $0, 1, 2, 5, 5, 6, 6, \mathbf{7}, 8, 8, 10, 12, 15, 30, 40$.
    • $\mathbf{\text{Median} = 7}$ (8th value)
    • $\mathbf{\text{Mean}} = \frac{155}{15} \approx \mathbf{10.33\text{ stories}}$
  • High Outliers ($30, 40$): Pull the mean upward! $$\mathbf{\text{Mean} > \text{Median}} \quad (10.33 > 7)$$
📰 Newspaper Pages (Low Outlier)
  • Pages from Mon to Sun: $16, 18, 20, 22, 26, 16, 10$.
  • Sorted: $10, 16, 16, \mathbf{18}, 20, 22, 26$.
    • $\mathbf{\text{Median} = 18\text{ pages}}$
    • $\mathbf{\text{Mean}} = \frac{128}{7} \approx \mathbf{18.29\text{ pages}}$
  • Three Distribution Rules:
    1. Balanced / Symmetrical $\implies \mathbf{\text{Mean} \approx \text{Median}}$
    2. Outlier on Lower End $\implies \mathbf{\text{Mean} < \text{Median}}$
    3. Outlier on Higher End $\implies \mathbf{\text{Mean} > \text{Median}}$
🎯 Measures of Central Tendency: Mean and Median both describe the "centre" where data values tend to cluster, each responding differently to skewness.

The "Zero Median" Mystery & Zero vs No Value

How a team can score 407 runs with a median of 0, and handling absent measurements
🏏 England's 407 Runs with Median = 0!
  • 11 Batsmen Scores:
    • Six Ducks ($0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0$)
    • Five scorers ($5, 19, 22, 158, 184$)
    • Extras: $19 \implies \text{Total} = 407/10$.
  • Sorted: $\mathbf{0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0}, 5, 19, 22, 158, 184$.
    The 6th value (middle) is strictly $\mathbf{0}$!
  • $\mathbf{\text{Median} = 0}$, yet $\mathbf{\text{Average}} = \frac{407 - 19}{11} = \mathbf{35.27\text{ runs}}$!
  • Shows that when the majority of data is zero, the median is zero regardless of massive outliers.
🚫 Zero vs "No Value" (Missing Data)
  • Zero is a Valid Score: If a player bats and scores 0 (a duck), they participated. Include $0$ and count that match in the denominator!
  • "Did Not Play" (Missing): If a player was absent/injured, do NOT count that match in the denominator.
    Scores: $57, 13, 0, 84, \text{[absent]}, 51, 27 \implies \text{Divide by } 6$, not $7$!
  • Seasonal Zeroes (Sita's Mangoes): For winter months ($0$ mangoes), do not average across all 12 months; calculate mean only across harvest months!

HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE JACOB KÖBEL (1500s)
PAGE 112

A Mean Foot: The 16-Man Churchyard Rod (1500s)

How 16th-century Europeans standardized the foot using the arithmetic mean before it had a name
🦶 The Medieval Land Measurement Crisis
  • In 16th-century Europe, agricultural land was surveyed using the standard unit: the Rod (defined as $16\text{ feet}$).
  • The Problem: A "foot" was literally the length of a human foot! But everyone's feet differed in length, sparking endless boundary disputes.
  • Whose foot should be the standard? The king's? The local mayor's?
⛪ Jacob Köbel's Statistical Solution
  • German surveyor Jacob Köbel established a community law:
    • On Sunday, stop the first 16 men leaving church.
    • Make them stand in a straight line, toe-to-heel.
    • The total length of these 16 feet was declared the official $16\text{-foot Rod}$!
  • Divide that rod into $16$ equal marks $\implies$ One standard "legal foot".
  • The Math: This was the exact arithmetic mean of 16 adult feet, eliminating individual variations!
🏛️ Historical Genius: By averaging across random individuals, natural variations cancel out, creating a stable, universally accepted scientific standard.

Master Data Handling Reference Checklist

Comprehensive conceptual recap of statistical thinking, central tendencies, and visual graphs
Central Tendencies & Outliers
  • ✅ Statistical Question: A question answered by data exhibiting variability.
  • ✅ Arithmetic Mean: $\text{Sum of values} \div \text{Total count}$. The physical "fair-share" levelling value (*Samīkaraṇa*).
  • ✅ Median: The middle number of sorted data. For even counts, average the two middle numbers.
  • ✅ Outlier Impact: Outliers heavily pull the Mean (low outlier $\implies \text{Mean} < \text{Median}$; high outlier $\implies \text{Mean} > \text{Median}$). The Median remains robust!
  • ✅ Zero vs Missing: Zero is an active data value; missing data must be excluded from the denominator.
Data Visualisation & Graphing
  • ✅ Dot Plots: Displays distribution, minimum, maximum, spread, clusters, and stacked frequencies along a line.
  • ✅ Double Column Graphs: Clustered bars sharing a common category axis, enabling dual comparison across time and categories.
  • ✅ 2-Step Analytical Method: Step 1 (Identify scales, legends, axes) $\to$ Step 2 (Infer trends, ratios, conclusions).
  • ✅ The 3-Digit Number Lock (Page 135): Using elimination of contradictory clues to deduce codes logically!
