5.1 Concept Intro

⚖️ The Balancing Act: Mean as a Physical Centre

Page 103–104
📖 Mean as the Centre of Gravity

The arithmetic mean represents the physical point of balance (fulcrum) of a dataset.

  • Two Points: For $3$ and $7$, the mean is $\frac{3+7}{2} = 5$. It lies exactly halfway: distance to $3$ is $2$, distance to $7$ is $2$.
  • Multiple Points: The mean is not necessarily the midpoint of the extreme values!
  • The Fundamental Balance Law: The sum of distances of values on the left (LHS) equals the sum of distances of values on the right (RHS) of the mean: $$\sum_{\text{left}} (\bar{x} - x_i) = \sum_{\text{right}} (x_j - \bar{x})$$

5.1 Mean Dynamics

🔄 Inserting and Removing Points: Tug of War

Page 105–106
1. Inserting Points
  • Value $> \bar{x}$: The mean increases (shifts right) to rebalance distances.
  • Value $< \bar{x}$: The mean decreases (shifts left).
  • Value $= \bar{x}$: The mean remains completely unchanged!
2. Removing Points
  • Remove Value $> \bar{x}$: The mean decreases (less weight on right).
  • Remove Value $< \bar{x}$: The mean increases.
  • Remove Value $= \bar{x}$: The mean stays identical.
3. Unchanging Mean Sets

Can we insert multiple values without changing the mean ($\bar{x} = 9$)?

Add two values below ($7$ and $7$, distances $2+2=4$) and one value above ($13$, distance $4$). $$\text{LHS distance} = \text{RHS distance} = 4$$ The mean stays exactly $9$!

5.1 Data Transformations

📈 Shifting and Scaling: Algebraic Invariants

Page 106–108
➕ Adding/Subtracting a Constant ($+k$ or $-k$)

If every observation in a collection increases by a constant $k$, the entire distribution slides rigidly along the axis:

$$\text{New Mean } = \bar{x} + k$$
  • Algebraic Proof: $$\frac{\sum (x_i + k)}{n} = \frac{\sum x_i + n \cdot k}{n} = \bar{x} + k$$
  • Shoe Height Example: Students wearing $1\text{ cm}$ thick shoes had average height $150.2\text{ cm}$. Without shoes, the true mean is $150.2 - 1 = \mathbf{149.2\text{ cm}}$!
✖️ Multiplying/Dividing by a Factor ($\times k$)

If every observation is scaled by a non-zero factor $k$:

$$\text{New Mean } = k \cdot \bar{x}$$
  • Algebraic Proof: $$\frac{\sum (k \cdot x_i)}{n} = k \cdot \frac{\sum x_i}{n} = k \cdot \bar{x}$$
  • If every player's weekly running mileage doubles ($\times 2$), the group mean running mileage doubles precisely.

5.1 Median Behavior

🎯 Tinkering with the Median: The Middle Value

Page 108–109
📖 Definition & Sorting Order

The median is the central value when data is sorted in ascending order.

  • For odd $N$: Exactly the $\frac{N+1}{2}$-th observation.
  • For even $N$: The arithmetic mean of the $\frac{N}{2}$-th and $\left(\frac{N}{2} + 1\right)$-th observations.
  • Robustness: Extreme outliers (e.g., $1000$ instead of $10$) do not shift the median, while they drastically distort the mean!
🔍 Response to New Entries
  • Original dataset: $7, 8, 9, 11, 13$ (Median $= 9$).
  • Add value $> \text{Median}$ (e.g., 14): $7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 14 \implies \text{Median} = \frac{9+11}{2} = \mathbf{10}$ (increases).
  • Add value $< \text{Median}$ (e.g., 5): $5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 13 \implies \text{Median} = \frac{8+9}{2} = \mathbf{8.5}$ (decreases).
  • Equal Entries: If we insert two values—one on each side of the median—the median remains unchanged.

5.1 Problem Solving

🔍 Finding the Unknown: Kushti Players & Coconut Trees

Page 109
🤼 Coach Balwan's Smudged Kushti Sheet

10 wrestlers' average weight was recorded as $39.2\text{ kg}$. Nine weights were legible: $42, 40, 39, 33, 48, 38, 42, 35, 32$.

$$\text{Total Sum} = 39.2 \times 10 = \mathbf{392\text{ kg}}$$ $$\text{Sum of 9 known weights} = \mathbf{349\text{ kg}}$$ $$\text{Missing weight } w = 392 - 349 = \mathbf{43\text{ kg}}$$
🥥 Venkayya's Coconut Harvest Error

15 coconut trees had an average harvest of $25.6$. One tree's yield was misread as 3 coconuts higher than actual.

$$\text{Initial Recorded Total} = 25.6 \times 15 = \mathbf{384}$$ $$\text{Correct Actual Total} = 384 - 3 = \mathbf{381}$$ $$\text{True Mean Harvest} = \frac{381}{15} = \mathbf{25.4\text{ coconuts/tree}}$$

5.1 Frequency Distributions

👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 Class Family Size: Frequency Weighted Analysis

Page 110
📊 Weighted Mean ($\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}$)

For 36 students, family sizes ($x_i$) occurred with frequencies ($f_i$):

$$\sum f_i x_i = (3 \times 3) + (4 \times 11) + (5 \times 9) + (6 \times 7) + (7 \times 3) + (8 \times 1) + (9 \times 1) + (10 \times 1)$$ $$= 9 + 44 + 45 + 42 + 21 + 8 + 9 + 10 = \mathbf{188}$$ $$\bar{x} = \frac{188}{36} \approx \mathbf{5.22\text{ members}}$$

Common Pitfall: Never average the distinct size values $\frac{3+4+\dots+10}{8} = 6.5$. You must weight each by its frequency!

🎯 Cumulative Frequency Median

With $N = 36$ values, the median is the average of the 18th and 19th ordered entries:

  • Size 3: Cumulative $= 3$
  • Size 4: Cumulative $= 3 + 11 = 14$ (positions 1–14)
  • Size 5: Cumulative $= 14 + 9 = 23$ (positions 15–23)
  • Both 18th and 19th values are 5!
  • Median Family Size $= \mathbf{5\text{ members}}$.

5.1 Digital Tools

💻 Spreadsheets: Automating Statistical Computation

Page 111–113
1. Cell Architecture

A spreadsheet is a grid of rows (numbered $1, 2, 3\dots$) and columns (lettered $A, B, C\dots$).

  • Intersection forms a cell address (e.g., $E5$ = Farooq's Maths mark).
  • Cells store labels (text), numeric data, or computational expressions.
2. Range Notation

Described using Start:End format:

  • B3:G3: Nagesh's marks across all 6 subjects in row 3.
  • D2:D6: English scores of the first 5 students in column D.
  • Enables bulk mathematical operations in a single step!
3. Formula Syntax

Always prefixed with an equals sign (=):

=SUM(B3:G3) : Computes Nagesh's total score.
=AVERAGE(G2:G23) : Computes class average in Science.
Instant recalculation if any cell value updates!

5.2 Trend Visualisation

📈 Line Graphs: Visualising Change Across Time

Page 116–118
🌡️ Clustered Column vs Line Graph

Comparing monthly maximum temperatures of Kerala and Punjab (2023):

  • Why Line Graphs Excel: When plotting continuous parameters over time, bar charts create visual clutter (e.g., 52 bars for 13 years of space launches!).
  • Lines connecting markers reveal rate of change via slope steepness (steeper $\implies$ faster change).
  • Distinct Markers: Circle vs. square markers allow unambiguous black-and-white print reading and accessibility.
🔍 Two-Step Interpretation Method
  • Step 1 (Identify What is Given): Note axis labels, units ($^\circ\text{C}$), time intervals (months), and legend keys.
  • Step 2 (Infer & Interpret Trends):
    • Punjab: Extreme range ($19^\circ\text{C}$ in Jan $\to 38^\circ\text{C}$ in June peak).
    • Kerala: Maritime stability ($29^\circ\text{C}$ to $33^\circ\text{C}$ flat curve all year).

Strategy Game

🎲 It's Puzzle Time! The Game of Hex

Page 132
📜 Rules of Hex (Piet Hein & John Nash)

Played on an $11 \times 11$ rhombus-shaped board composed of interlocking hexagonal cells:

  • Two players are assigned opposite pairs of board sides (e.g., Red vs. Blue).
  • Players take turns placing a colored marker on any vacant hexagon.
  • Markers can never be moved, jumped, or captured.
  • Objective: Construct an unbroken connected chain of your colored stones joining your two assigned opposite borders!
🧠 Mathematical Significance
  • No Draws Theorem: Mathematician John Nash proved that a game of Hex can never end in a tie! One player must eventually connect their borders.
  • First-Player Win: Using a strategy-stealing argument, the first player theoretically has a guaranteed winning strategy.
  • Combines graph connectivity, topology, and discrete geometric logic.

Exercise Highlights

⭐ Key Worked Exemplars: Mean & Median Calculations

Page 113–115
1. Missing Dot for Mean 9

Known dots: $4, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 9, 9, 9, 11$. Known sum $= 84$ ($n=10$). Find missing 11th value for $\bar{x} = 9$.

$$\text{Required Total} = 11 \times 9 = 99$$ $$\text{Missing dot } x = 99 - 84 = \mathbf{15}$$ Checks balance: LHS distance $(5+2+1+1) = 9$; RHS distance $(2+4) + x_{\text{dist}} = 9 \implies \mathbf{15}$.
2. First 50 Multiples of 4

Find the mean of $4, 8, 12, \dots, 200$.

Factor out 4: $$\bar{x} = 4 \times \left(\frac{1 + 2 + \dots + 50}{50}\right)$$ $$= 4 \times \frac{50 \times 51}{2 \times 50} = 4 \times 25.5 = \mathbf{102}$$ Equivalently: midpoint $\frac{4 + 200}{2} = \mathbf{102}$!
3. Median Constrained Entry

Data: $8, 8, 12, 18, 25, 29, 35, 39, 47, 73, p$ ($11$ values). If median is $29$, what values can $p$ take?

For median to be the 6th value $29$: There must be 5 values $\le 29$. The 5 known values $\le 29$ are $8, 8, 12, 18, 25$. Therefore, $p$ can be any value $\ge 29$!

Chapter Summary

📋 Master Summary Checklist

Page 133
✅ Central Tendency Mechanics
  • Mean as a Fulcrum: The unique balance point where total distance on the left equals total distance on the right ($\sum \Delta x_{\text{left}} = \sum \Delta x_{\text{right}}$).
  • Insertion Rule: Values $> \bar{x}$ increase the mean; values $< \bar{x}$ decrease the mean; values $= \bar{x}$ leave the mean unaffected.
  • Rigid Shift & Scaling: Adding $k$ shifts mean to $\bar{x} + k$; multiplying by $k$ scales mean to $k\bar{x}$.
  • Median Resilience: Middle value in ranked data; unaffected by distant outlier values.
  • Weighted Frequencies: $\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}$; median tracked via cumulative frequency counts.
✅ Visual Analytics & Digital Spreadsheets
  • Spreadsheets: Rectangular grid of cells referenced as Column-Row ($E5$); automated range functions (=SUM(), =AVERAGE()).
  • Line Graphs: Optimal format for tracking continuous changes across time; slope gradient reflects rate of change.
  • Two-Step Protocol: Step 1 (identify axes, scales, legends) $\to$ Step 2 (infer trends, inflection points, and causes).
  • Climatic Case Studies: Contrast between extreme continental ranges (Punjab) and maritime moderation (Kerala).
  • Activity Strips: Chronological 48-box representations translating daily schedules into comparative visual patterns.
