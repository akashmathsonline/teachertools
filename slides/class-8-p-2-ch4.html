4.1 Fractals Intro

🌿 Fractals: Self-Similarity in Nature

Page 70
📖 What is a Fractal?

A fractal is a geometric shape that exhibits self-similarity: it repeats the same or very similar pattern over and over again at smaller and smaller scales.

  • The Fern Frond: Each leaf of a fern is a smaller replica of the entire fern; sub-leaflets replicate the leaf shape at an even smaller scale!
  • Branching Trees: The main trunk splits into thick limbs, which divide into branches, then branchlets, and twigs.
  • Other Natural Fractals: Cumulus clouds, coastline jaggedness, mountain ridges, and lightning forks.

4.1 Mathematical Fractals

🔲 The Sierpiński Carpet: Step-by-Step Construction

Page 70–71
📜 The Construction Rule (Wacław Sierpiński)

Discovered by Polish mathematician Wacław Sierpiński (1916):

  • Step 0: Start with a single solid square ($R_0 = 1, H_0 = 0$).
  • Step 1: Partition the square into $3 \times 3 = 9$ congruent mini-squares and remove the central square hole ($R_1 = 8, H_1 = 1$).
  • Step 2: Repeat the exact process on every remaining square ($R_2 = 8 \times 8 = 64$).
  • The process repeats infinitely, creating a lace-like square with zero area!

4.1 Mathematical Analysis

🔢 Sierpiński Carpet: Squares, Holes & Area Growth

Page 71
1. Remaining Squares ($R_n$)

Each remaining square spawns 8 smaller squares in the next step:

$$R_n = 8^n$$
  • $R_0 = 1$
  • $R_1 = 8^1 = 8$
  • $R_2 = 8^2 = 64$
  • $R_3 = 8^3 = 512$
2. Number of Holes ($H_n$)

Prior holes stay, plus 1 new hole per remaining square:

$$H_{n+1} = H_n + R_n$$
  • $H_1 = 1$
  • $H_2 = 1 + 8 = 9$
  • $H_3 = 1 + 8 + 8^2 = 73$
  • Formula: $H_n = \frac{8^n - 1}{7}$
3. Remaining Area ($A_n$)

Each step retains $\frac{8}{9}$ of the area from the prior step:

$$A_n = \left(\frac{8}{9}\right)^n$$

As $n \to \infty$, $\left(\frac{8}{9}\right)^n \to 0$. The infinite carpet has zero area, but infinite boundary length!

4.1 Triangular Fractals

🔺 The Sierpiński Gasket (Sierpiński Triangle)

Page 72
📐 Midpoint Division Principle

Start with an equilateral triangle of area $1$:

  • Join the midpoints of the 3 sides. This partitions the parent triangle into 4 identical congruent equilateral triangles.
  • Remove the central upside-down triangle (leaving 3 shaded triangles).
  • Repeat this process on all 3 remaining triangles at each step.
  • Triangles remaining at step $n$: $T_n = 3^n$.
  • Area remaining at step $n$: $A_n = \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^n$.

4.1 Snowflake Fractal

❄️ The Koch Snowflake: Infinite Perimeter, Finite Area

Page 73
📜 The Generation Algorithm (Helge von Koch, 1904)

Constructed starting with an equilateral triangle of side $1$:

  • Divide each line segment into 3 equal parts (each length $\frac{1}{3}$).
  • Erect an equilateral triangle on the middle third, then delete the base of that triangle.
  • Every single line segment is replaced by 4 segments of $\frac{1}{3}$ length.
  • Sides at Step $n$: $N_n = 3 \times 4^n$.
  • Perimeter at Step $n$: $P_n = 3 \times \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^n$.

🦎 M.C. Escher's Art

"Smaller and Smaller" (1956): Dutch graphic artist M.C. Escher populated woodcuts with interlocking lizards that shrink systematically by precise scale factors toward the center of the artwork.

4.2 Solid Geometry

📦 Faces, Edges & Vertices: Prisms vs Pyramids

Page 78–79
1. Anatomy of Polyhedra
  • Faces ($F$): Flat polygonal boundary surfaces.
  • Edges ($E$): Line segments where 2 faces meet.
  • Vertices ($V$): Corner points where edges meet.
  • Cube/Cuboid: $F=6, E=12, V=8$.
2. Prisms ($n$-gon base)

Two congruent parallel $n$-gon bases joined by rectangular/parallelogram walls:

$$F = n + 2, \quad E = 3n, \quad V = 2n$$

A 10-gon prism has $12$ faces, $30$ edges, $20$ vertices.

3. Pyramids ($n$-gon base)

One $n$-gon base with triangular lateral faces meeting at an apex:

$$F = n + 1, \quad E = 2n, \quad V = n + 1$$

A 10-gon pyramid has $11$ faces, $20$ edges, $11$ vertices.

4.2 Nets of Solids

✂️ Unfolding Solids into 2D Nets

Page 79–83
📦 Nets of Familiar Solids
  • Net: A 2D planar figure of connected faces that folds into a closed 3D solid without overlaps.
  • Cube: Exactly 11 distinct nets exist (excluding rotations/reflections).
  • Regular Tetrahedron: Exactly 2 nets exist (made of 4 equilateral triangles).
  • Cylinder: A rectangle of length $2\pi r$ and width $h$, plus 2 circular end caps.
  • Cone: A circular sector of radius $l$ with arc length $2\pi r$, plus 1 base disc.
💎 Higher Regular Polyhedra Nets
  • Octahedron (8 triangular faces): Two square pyramids glued at their base. Has exactly 11 distinct nets (just like the cube!).
  • Dodecahedron (12 pentagonal faces): Made entirely of regular pentagons. Mathematicians proved it has exactly 43,380 distinct nets!
  • Sphere: Has no flat net! Cannot be flattened without stretching, tearing, or wrinkling.

4.2 Optimization

🐜 Shortest Paths on a Cuboid: Unfolding to a Net

Page 84–86
🍬 The Ant and the Laddu Problem

An ant crawls strictly on the outer surface of a box to reach a sweet laddu:

  • On a 2D flat plane, the shortest distance between two points is a straight line.
  • Transformation Principle: Unfold the 3D surface into a flat 2D net. The shortest path on the surface corresponds to the straight line segment on the net!
  • Warning: A line that cuts across empty space outside the net does NOT correspond to a valid surface path!

4.2 Rigorous Modeling

📐 The Tricky Case: Comparing Different Unfoldings

Page 86–87
📦 Box Dimensions ($30 \times 12 \times 12\text{ cm}$)

Ant is $1\text{ cm}$ below ceiling on right wall; Laddu is $1\text{ cm}$ above floor on left wall:

  • Unfolding Way 1 (Across end walls): $$\text{Total span} = 6 + 30 + 6 = \mathbf{42\text{ cm}}$$
  • Unfolding Way 2 (Across top & side faces): Form right triangle with legs $32\text{ cm}$ and $24\text{ cm}$: $$d^2 = 32^2 + 24^2 = 1024 + 576 = 1600$$ $$\mathbf{d = \sqrt{1600} = 40\text{ cm}}$$
  • $40\text{ cm} < 42\text{ cm}$! Unfolding across top & side is strictly shorter!
💡 Critical Mathematical Insight

Different net unfoldings yield different straight-line lengths!

$$\text{Shortest Path} = \min(d_1, d_2, d_3, \dots)$$
  • One must test all geometrically viable nets of the cuboid.
  • Calculate diagonal lengths using Baudhāyana-Pythagoras theorem: $d = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2}$.

4.2 Projection Theory

☀️ Perpendicular Projections & Sunlight Shadows

Page 88–90, 94
🔦 Defining Orthogonal Projection

Drop perpendicular lines from every point of an object in 3D space onto a reference plane $M$:

  • The point $O$ where line $PO \perp \text{Plane } M$ is the projection of point $P$.
  • Line Segment Projection: If a segment of length $l$ is inclined, its projected length $p \le l$. Projected length equals actual length ($p = l$) if and only if the line is parallel to the plane!
  • Parallel Lines Invariance: The projection of any pair of parallel lines is always parallel!
☀️ The Sun as a Universal Projector
  • A nearby flashlight casts divergent shadows that are magnified or distorted.
  • As the light source moves infinitely far away, the rays become strictly parallel.
  • Because the Sun is roughly 150 million km away, sunlight shadows falling perpendicular to a wall are exact orthogonal projections!

4.2 Multi-View Engineering

📐 Multi-View Engineering: Front, Top & Side Views

Page 91–93
1. Vertical Plane (Front)

Projection onto a plane standing in front of the solid. Represents the Front View (Elevation). Shows true height and length.

2. Horizontal Plane (Top)

Projection looking straight down from directly above onto the floor. Represents the Top View (Plan). Shows true length and depth.

3. Side Plane (Profile)

Projection onto a plane positioned to the side of the solid. Represents the Side View. Shows true depth and height.

$$\text{Three mutually perpendicular 2D views uniquely reconstruct any complex 3D object without ambiguity!}$$

4.2 Isometric Drawing

🧊 Isometric Projections & The Hexagonal Grid

Page 97–101
⚖️ "Isometric" = Equal Measure

Balance a cube on one of its corner vertices and project it downward:

  • The outer perimeter of the projection forms a regular hexagon!
  • All 12 edges project with equal length along 3 families of parallel lines: vertical ($|$), forward-slant ($\backslash$), and backward-slant ($/$).
  • These correspond directly to the 3 spatial axes: Height, Length, and Depth.
  • Tiling the plane with these lines forms the standard isometric grid used in engineering design.

$$\text{Top layer} = 1$$ $$\text{Middle layer} = 3$$ $$\text{Bottom layer} = 6$$ $$\text{Total} = 1 + 3 + 6 = \mathbf{10\text{ cubes}}$$ (Triangular number sum!)
3. Impossible Penrose Triangle

Why does the illusion work?

Locally, each of the three corner joints is a valid $90^\circ$ right angle. Globally, 3D space forbids three $90^\circ$ turns forming a closed triangle!
