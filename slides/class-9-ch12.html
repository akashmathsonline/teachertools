Section 12.1 • Foundations

What Exactly is a Quadrilateral?

Pages 52–54
Definition 1 (Textbook Standard)

Suppose $A, B, C, D$ are four distinct points in a plane. Then the points on the 4 segments $AB, BC, CD$ and $DA$ form quadrilateral $ABCD$ if every such point other than $A, B, C,$ and $D$ lies on exactly one of these four segments.

1. No Three Collinear

Rules out figures like NOPE (which becomes a triangle) and SILY (which collapses into overlapping line segments).

2. Planar Condition

All four vertices must lie in a single plane. Four-sided skew figures in 3D (like BENT) are termed non-planar quadrilaterals.

3. Non-Self-Intersecting

Rules out crossing figures like CUTS. Points on edges must belong strictly to one side, preventing interior crossings.

Section 12.1 • Classification

Convex vs. Non-Convex Quadrilaterals

Pages 54–55
Convex Quadrilaterals
  • Every internal angle is strictly less than $180^\circ$ ($\angle < 180^\circ$).
  • Both diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ intersect completely in the interior.
  • Connecting any two points inside produces a segment lying entirely inside.
Non-Convex (Concave) 4-Gons
  • Contains a "dent" at one vertex where the internal reflex angle is $> 180^\circ$ (e.g. DART).
  • The diagonals do not intersect inside the figure.
  • Commonly found in mechanical four-bar linkages and robotic kinematics!
Naming Conventions

Quadrilateral $ABCD$ can only be traced cyclically around the perimeter: $BCDA, CDAB, DABC, DCBA, ADCB, BADC,$ or $CBAD$. Any crossing sequence (e.g., $ACBD$) is invalid.

Section 12.2 • Parallelograms

Parallelograms: Forward & Converse Theorems

Pages 56–58
Theorem 1: Forward Properties
  • Opposite sides are equal: $AB = DC$ and $AD = BC$.
  • Opposite angles are equal: $\angle A = \angle C$ and $\angle B = \angle D$.
  • Diagonals bisect each other: $EA = EC$ and $EB = ED$.
Theorem 2: Equal Opposite Sides

Converse of (a): If both pairs of opposite sides are equal, the quadrilateral is a parallelogram.

Proof Strategy: Draw diagonal $AC$. By SSS criterion, $\Delta ACD \cong \Delta CAB$. Alternate interior angles match, proving $AB \parallel DC$ and $AD \parallel BC$.

Theorem 3: Equal Opposite Angles Test

If opposite angles are equal ($\angle A = \angle C = x$ and $\angle B = \angle D = y$), then $2x + 2y = 360^\circ \implies x + y = 180^\circ$. Because consecutive angles are supplementary, transversal lines prove opposite sides are strictly parallel.

Section 12.2 • Recognition Criteria

Testing Parallelograms: Diagonals & The Key Pair

Pages 59–60
Theorem 4: Diagonals Bisect Test

Converse of (c): A quadrilateral whose diagonals bisect each other is a parallelogram.

Proof: Let $E$ be the intersection of diagonals $AC$ and $BD$. Since $EA = EC$, $EB = ED$, and $\angle AED = \angle CEB$ (vertically opposite), $\Delta AED \cong \Delta CEB$ by SAS. Hence $\angle DAE = \angle ECB$, proving $AD \parallel BC$.

Theorem 5: Equal & Parallel Sides Test

The Golden Shortcut: A quadrilateral with one pair of opposite sides that are both equal and parallel is a parallelogram.

Proof: If $AB \parallel DC$ and $AB = DC$, drawing diagonal $AC$ creates alternating angles $\angle BAC = \angle DCA$. With shared $AC$, $\Delta CAB \cong \Delta ACD$ by SAS, yielding $AD = BC$ and $AD \parallel BC$.

Section 12.3.1 • Triangle Properties

Theorem 6: The Midpoint Theorem

Pages 61–63
Theorem 6 Statement

The segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and has half its length.

If $P$ is midpoint of $AB$ and $Q$ is midpoint of $AC \implies PQ \parallel BC$ and $PQ = \frac{1}{2}BC$
Auxiliary Construction Proof
  • Extend $PQ$ to point $R$ such that line $CR \parallel BA$.
  • In $\Delta APQ$ and $\Delta CRQ$: $AQ = QC$, $\angle AQP = \angle CQR$, and $\angle PAQ = \angle RCQ$.
  • $\therefore \Delta APQ \cong \Delta CRQ$ by ASA/AAS.
  • This gives $CR = AP = PB$ and $PQ = QR$.
Applying Theorem 5
  • Since $CR = PB$ and $CR \parallel PB$, quadrilateral $BCRP$ is a parallelogram.
  • Thus, line $PR \parallel BC \implies PQ \parallel BC$.
  • Also $PR = BC$. Because $PQ = \frac{1}{2}PR$, we get $PQ = \frac{1}{2}BC$.

Section 12.3.1 • Triangle Properties

Theorem 7: Converse of the Midpoint Theorem

Pages 63–64
Theorem 7 Statement

The line drawn through the midpoint of one side of a triangle and parallel to another side bisects the third side.

Proof 1: Backward Parallelogram

Draw line through $C$ parallel to $BA$ meeting $PQ$ extended at $R$. Since $PR \parallel BC$ and $CR \parallel BP$, quadrilateral $BCRP$ is a parallelogram. Thus $CR = BP = AP$. Using $\Delta APQ \cong \Delta CRQ$ (ASA), we deduce $AQ = QC$.

Proof 2: Uniqueness of Parallels

Suppose midpoint of $AC$ is $M$. By Theorem 6, $PM \parallel BC$. But through point $P$, Euclid's parallel postulate guarantees there is only one unique line parallel to $BC$. Thus line $PM$ must coincide with line $PQ$. Hence $M = Q$.

Section 12.3.2 • Triangle Geometry

Theorem 8: Concurrency of Medians & Centroid

Pages 65–66
Theorem 8 (Centroid Theorem)

The three medians of $\Delta ABC$ pass through a single common point (are concurrent), which divides each median in the ratio $2:1$, with the longer part connecting to the vertex.

The Clever Bisection Idea
  • Let medians $CP$ and $BQ$ intersect at $M$.
  • Bisect $BM$ at $X$ and $CM$ at $Y$.
  • In $\Delta ABC$: $PQ \parallel BC$ and $PQ = \frac{1}{2}BC$ (Midpoint Thm).
  • In $\Delta MBC$: $XY \parallel BC$ and $XY = \frac{1}{2}BC$ (Midpoint Thm).
  • $\therefore PQ \parallel XY$ and $PQ = XY$.
Parallelogram & Concurrency
  • $PQXY$ is a parallelogram! Its diagonals bisect each other.
  • $\implies MP = MY = YC \implies CM = 2MP$.
  • Likewise, $MQ = MX = XB \implies BM = 2MQ$.
  • Repeating with medians $AR$ and $BQ$ shows they intersect at point $N$ dividing $BQ$ as $2:1$.
  • Since only one point divides $BQ$ in ratio $2:1$, $M$ and $N$ must be identical!

Section 12.3.2 • Quadrilateral Theorems

Theorem 9: Varignon's Parallelogram

Pages 67–68
Theorem 9 Statement

The midpoints of the four sides of ANY quadrilateral are the vertices of a parallelogram. This inscribed shape is known as the Varignon parallelogram.

The Diagonal Split Proof
  • Let $P, Q, R, S$ be the midpoints of sides $AB, BC, CD, DA$.
  • Draw diagonal $AC$. In $\Delta ABC$, $PQ \parallel AC$ and $PQ = \frac{1}{2}AC$.
  • In $\Delta ADC$, $SR \parallel AC$ and $SR = \frac{1}{2}AC$.
  • Thus, $PQ \parallel SR$ and $PQ = SR$. By Theorem 5, $PQRS$ is a parallelogram!
Geometric Corollaries
  • The segments joining opposite midpoints ($PR$ and $QS$) bisect each other.
  • If diagonals of $ABCD$ are equal, $PQRS$ is a rhombus.
  • If diagonals of $ABCD$ are perpendicular, $PQRS$ is a rectangle.
  • If diagonals are both equal and perpendicular, $PQRS$ is a square!

Section 12.4 • Tessellations

Tiling the Plane Using Any Arbitrary 4-Gon

Pages 69–73
The Fundamental Principle of Quadrilateral Tiling

Because the internal angles of any planar non-self-intersecting 4-gon sum to $360^\circ$ ($\angle 1 + \angle 2 + \angle 3 + \angle 4 = 360^\circ$), four identical copies can meet at every vertex with each angle appearing exactly once, tiling the entire plane seamlessly!

Method 1: Half-Turn Rotations
  • Take an arbitrary quadrilateral $SOME$.
  • Rotate a copy by $180^\circ$ about the midpoint of edge $OM$.
  • The copy aligns seamlessly with the original along $OM$.
  • Repeating this $180^\circ$ rotation about the midpoints of all edges places 4 copies around each corner!
Method 2: The Varignon Grid
  • Every quadrilateral contains a Varignon parallelogram.
  • Start by tiling the plane with a standard grid of Varignon parallelograms.
  • Place copies of $SOME$ aligned with the shaded parallelograms.
  • The remaining gaps automatically form congruent inverted copies of $SOME$!

Modern Spotlight • Mathematical Discovery

From Quasicrystals to the 2023 "Hat" Monotile

Pages 73–74
Quasicrystals & Dan Shechtman
  • Mathematicians created aperiodic tilings (such as Penrose tiles) that cover the plane without ever repeating.
  • Dan Shechtman discovered physical materials with this non-repeating structure, winning the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry!
The 2023 "Hat" Einstein Monotile
  • For decades, mathematicians asked: can a single shape tile the plane aperiodically? (An "einstein" tile).
  • In 2023, Smith, Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss discovered the 13-sided polygon known as "the hat".
  • It tiles the plane endlessly with zero periodic translation, solving a major open problem in geometry!
Why Regular Pentagons Fail to Tile

Each internal angle of a regular pentagon is $\frac{(5-2) \times 180^\circ}{5} = 108^\circ$. At any common junction point, $3 \times 108^\circ = 324^\circ$ leaves a $36^\circ$ gap. Because $360^\circ$ is not divisible by $108^\circ$, regular pentagons cannot tile the plane!

Exercise Highlights Worked Examples

Worked Solutions (1 & 2)

1. Diagonal Bisecting Angle (Ex 12.2 Q2)

Question: In a parallelogram $ABCD$, diagonal $AC$ bisects $\angle A$. Show that it bisects $\angle C$ and that $ABCD$ is a rhombus.

  • $AC$ bisects $\angle A \implies \angle BAC = \angle DAC$.
  • Since $AB \parallel CD$, $\angle BAC = \angle DCA$ (alternate interior).
  • Since $AD \parallel BC$, $\angle DAC = \angle BCA$ (alternate interior).
  • $\therefore \angle DCA = \angle BCA$, proving $AC$ bisects $\angle C$.
  • In $\Delta ABC$, $\angle BAC = \angle BCA \implies AB = BC$.
  • A parallelogram with adjacent sides equal is a rhombus!
2. Trapezium Midline (End Ex Q7)

Question: In trapezium $ABCD$ ($AB \parallel CD$), $E$ is the midpoint of $AD$ and $EF \parallel AB$. Show that $F$ is the midpoint of $BC$ and $EF = \frac{AB + CD}{2}$.

  • Join diagonal $BD$, intersecting $EF$ at point $M$.
  • In $\Delta DAB$, $E$ is midpoint of $AD$ and $EM \parallel AB$. By Converse Midpoint Thm, $M$ is midpoint of $BD$, and $EM = \frac{1}{2}AB$.
  • In $\Delta BDC$, $M$ is midpoint of $BD$ and $MF \parallel DC$. By Converse Midpoint Thm, $F$ is midpoint of $BC$, and $MF = \frac{1}{2}CD$.
  • $EF = EM + MF = \frac{1}{2}AB + \frac{1}{2}CD = \frac{AB + CD}{2}$.

Worked Solutions (3)

Problem 3: Trisection of Diagonal AC (End-of-Chapter Q12)

Statement: Let $ABCD$ be a parallelogram, and let $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of sides $AB$ and $CD$ respectively. Show that segments $DM$ and $BN$ trisect diagonal $AC$.

  • 1. Quadrilateral $MBND$: Since $ABCD$ is a parallelogram, $AB = CD$ and $AB \parallel CD$. $MB = \frac{1}{2}AB$ and $DN = \frac{1}{2}CD$. So $MB = DN$ and $MB \parallel DN$. By Theorem 5, $MBND$ is a parallelogram $\implies DM \parallel BN$.
  • 2. In $\Delta ABY$: Let $DM$ intersect $AC$ at $X$, and $BN$ intersect $AC$ at $Y$. $M$ is midpoint of $AB$, and $MX \parallel BY$. By Converse Midpoint Thm, $X$ is midpoint of $AY \implies AX = XY$.
  • 3. In $\Delta CDX$: $N$ is midpoint of $CD$, and $NY \parallel DX$. By Converse Midpoint Thm, $Y$ is midpoint of $CX \implies XY = YC$.
  • Conclusion: $AX = XY = YC$. Hence, $DM$ and $BN$ trisect $AC$.
