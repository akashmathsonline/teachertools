14.1 Cuboids & Cubes

🧱 3D Geometry of Cuboids and Cubes

Page 123–124
📦 Anatomy of a Cuboid

A cuboid is the 3-dimensional analogue of a rectangle. It possesses 6 rectangular faces, 12 edges, and 8 vertices, characterised by length $l$, width $w$, and height $h$.

$$\text{TSA} = 2(lw + wh + lh)$$
  • Net Construction: Unfolding the 6 connected rectangles demonstrates that opposite faces are congruent pairs with areas $lw$, $wh$, and $lh$.
  • Cube as a Special Case ($l = w = h = a$):
  • $\text{Total Surface Area} = 6a^2$
  • $\text{Volume of Cube} = a^3$

14.1 Volume Derivation

🃏 The "Stack of Cards" Volume Model

Page 124–125
🎴 The Intuitive Physical Model

Consider a pack of playing cards. Each individual card has a 2D base area of length $l \times$ width $w$. As identical cards are stacked to a vertical height $h$:

$$V = \text{Base Area} \times \text{Height} = (l \times w) \times h = lwh$$
  • Unit Cube Definition: Volume is defined by how many unit cubes ($1\text{ cm}^3$ or $1\text{ m}^3$) fit into the space.
  • Cavalieri's Spirit: Shearing or slanting the stack shifts the outer shape, but the total volume of cards remains identical.
⚖️ Volume vs Surface Area Trade-off

Textbook Example 1: Compare Cube A ($a = 6\text{ cm}$) and Cuboid B ($9 \times 6 \times 4\text{ cm}$):

$$V_A = 6^3 = 216\text{ cm}^3, \quad \text{SA}_A = 6(6^2) = 216\text{ cm}^2$$ $$V_B = 9 \times 6 \times 4 = 216\text{ cm}^3$$ $$\text{SA}_B = 2(54 + 24 + 36) = 228\text{ cm}^2$$

📌 Conclusion: For identical holding capacity, a cube minimizes surface area (material cost/heat loss), whereas flattened cuboids maximize environmental contact.

14.2 Cylinders

🥫 Right Circular Cylinder: Anatomy & Slicing

Page 127–128
📐 "Right" vs "Oblique"

Circular: Cross-section parallel to the base is a circle.
Right: The central rotational axis is strictly perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to its circular bases.
Oblique cylinders have tilted axes and are outside our curriculum.

🔬 Cross-Section Concept

An infinitely thin slice through an object:

  • Slice parallel to base $\implies$ Circle
  • Slice parallel to axis $\implies$ Rectangle
  • Real-world: MRI medical body scans slice 3D anatomy into continuous 2D planar images.
🥞 "Khakhra / Coin" Stack

Just as cards form a cuboid, stacking identical thin circular discs (like coins or thin khakhras) of area $\pi r^2$ to height $h$ yields: $$\text{Volume} = (\pi r^2) \times h = \pi r^2 h$$

14.2 Surface Area

📜 Unrolling the Curved Surface of a Cylinder

Page 128–129
✂️ Unrolling into a Flat Rectangle

Slitting the curved wall vertically and rolling it flat opens into a rectangle whose:

  • Length: Boundary circumference of base $= 2\pi r$
  • Breadth: Height of cylinder $= h$
$$\text{Curved Surface Area (CSA)} = 2\pi rh$$

Savitri's Project (Example 2): Kaleidoscope chart paper required with $r = 3.5\text{ cm}$, $h = 25\text{ cm}$: $$\text{CSA} = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 3.5 \times 25 = 550\text{ cm}^2$$

🏷️ The Three Boundary Conditions
  • 1. Open Pipe / Hollow Tube:
    $\text{Surface Area} = \text{CSA} = \mathbf{2\pi rh}$
  • 2. Tumbler / Open Top Bucket:
    One base closed: $\text{SA} = 2\pi rh + \pi r^2 = \mathbf{\pi r(2h + r)}$
  • 3. Closed Tin / Canister:
    Both circular lids closed:
    $\text{TSA} = 2\pi rh + 2\pi r^2 = \mathbf{2\pi r(h + r)}$

14.3 Cones

🍦 Cones: Revolution & The Triangle Relationship

Page 130–131
🔄 Solid of Revolution

A right circular cone is generated when a right-angled triangle is rotated $360^\circ$ about one of its perpendicular legs.

  • Base Radius ($r$): Rotating horizontal leg.
  • Vertical Height ($h$): Axis of rotation.
  • Slant Height ($l$): Rotating hypotenuse.
$$l^2 = h^2 + r^2 \iff l = \sqrt{h^2 + r^2}$$

(Directly derived from Baudhayana-Pythagoras theorem).

14.3 Area Derivation

📐 Derivation of Cone Curved Surface Area ($\pi r l$)

Page 130–131
Approach 1: Sum of Narrow Triangles

Unroll the cone along slant edge $l$. Slice the sector into $n$ narrow isosceles triangles with bases $b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n$ and altitude $l$:

$$\text{CSA} = \frac{1}{2}b_1 l + \frac{1}{2}b_2 l + \dots + \frac{1}{2}b_n l$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}(b_1 + b_2 + \dots + b_n)l$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}(\text{base perimeter})l = \frac{1}{2}(2\pi r)l = \mathbf{\pi rl}$$
Approach 2: Sector of a Circle Ratio

The unrolled surface is a circle sector of radius $l$ subtending angle $\theta$:

$$\text{Arc length } s = 2\pi r = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 2\pi l \implies \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} = \frac{r}{l}$$ $$\text{Area} = \pi l^2 \times \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} = \pi l^2 \left(\frac{r}{l}\right) = \mathbf{\pi rl}$$
$$\text{TSA} = \pi rl + \pi r^2 = \pi r(l + r)$$

14.3 Cone Volume

🧪 Why the $\frac{1}{3}$ Factor? Hands-on Experiments

Page 132–133
$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3} \times \text{Base Area} \times \text{Height}$$
🧂 Experiment 1: The Salt Pouring Test

Construct a hollow cone and a cylinder having identical base radius $r$ and height $h$:

  • Fill the cone to the brim with fine salt $\to$ pour into cylinder ($1/3$ full).
  • Repeat a second time $\to$ cylinder is $2/3$ full.
  • Repeat a third time $\to$ cylinder is filled exactly to the brim!
  • Empirical Law: $\text{Vol of Cylinder} = 3 \times \text{Vol of Cone}$.
🏺 Experiment 2: Modelling Clay & Archimedes

Mold plasticine clay into a cylinder of dimensions $r$ and $h$. Reshape the exact same clay into cones of identical $r$ and $h$. You will obtain exactly 3 cones!

Archimedes (c. 225 BCE): First rigorously proved this ratio geometrically eighteen centuries prior to modern integral calculus.

14.4 Pyramids

🏔️ Pyramidal Shapes in Geometry & Nature

Page 134–135
📐 Generalized Apex Geometry

A pyramid consists of any polygonal base connected to a single point (the apex) lying in a different plane:

  • Square Base: The Great Pyramids of Giza (Egypt).
  • Triangular Base (Tetrahedron): Rubik's Pyraminx puzzle.
  • Connection to Cone: A cone is simply a pyramid whose base is a circle (infinite sides)!
$$V = \frac{1}{3} \times \text{Base Area} \times \text{Height}$$
🏔️ Glacial Pyramidal Peaks

In geography and geomorphology, cirque glaciers erode mountainsides on 3 or 4 sides over millennia, carving sharp pyramid peaks:

  • Shivling Peak: Uttarakhand Himalayas, India.
  • The Matterhorn: Pennine Alps, Switzerland.
  • Surface Area: Sum of the base polygon plus all triangular lateral faces.

14.5 Spheres

⚽ The Sphere & Archimedes' Circumscribed Cylinder

Page 135–136
📜 Archimedes' Greatest Discovery

Archimedes requested a sphere inside its tightly fitting circumscribed cylinder engraved on his tombstone:

  • Sphere radius $= r$
  • Cylinder radius $= r$, and Cylinder height $h = 2r$
  • $\text{Cylinder CSA} = 2\pi r h = 2\pi r(2r) = \mathbf{4\pi r^2}$
  • Theorem: The surface area of the sphere is identically equal to the curved area of this enclosing cylinder!
$$\text{Surface Area of Sphere} = 4\pi r^2$$
🗺️ Cartography & Map Projections

This exact theorem is the basis of the Cylindrical (Equirectangular) Map Projection:

  • Projecting radially outward from the Earth's polar axis onto a wrapped cylinder unrolls into a rectangular world map.
  • Trade-off: Preserves equatorial shapes with high fidelity, but causes severe area distortion near the poles (e.g., Greenland appears gigantic).

14.5.1 Sphere Verification

🧶 String Winding Activity & Cone Decomposition

Page 137–139
🧶 The 4-Circle String Experiment

Wind a string tightly to cover a rubber ball of radius $r$. Mark the length and unwind.

  • Draw flat circles of radius $r$ on paper.
  • The unwound string from the sphere exactly fills four full circles!
  • $\text{Area} = 4 \times (\pi r^2) = \mathbf{4\pi r^2}$.
🔺 Visualizing Volume: Tiny Cones

Decompose the sphere surface into thousands of tiny polygonal patches of area $\Delta A_i$. Connect each to the center $O$:

$$V = \sum \frac{1}{3}(\Delta A_i) r = \frac{r}{3} \sum \Delta A_i = \frac{r}{3} (4\pi r^2) = \mathbf{\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3}$$

This provides an intuitive, pre-calculus geometric proof of the sphere's volume formula!

14.5.2 Hemispheres

🥣 Hemispheres: Curved vs Total Surface Area

Page 139–141
1. Curved Surface (CSA)

Exactly half the curved surface of a full sphere:

$$\text{CSA} = \frac{1}{2}(4\pi r^2) = 2\pi r^2$$

Applied for domes, bowl exteriors, and igloo roofs where the base is open to the ground.

2. Total Surface Area (TSA)

Curved hemisphere plus flat circular base cut:

$$\text{TSA} = 2\pi r^2 + \pi r^2 = 3\pi r^2$$

Crucial for solid hemispheres (e.g., solid wooden bowls, fruit halves).

3. Hemispherical Volume

Half the cubic content of a full sphere:

$$V = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\right) = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$$

Example: A $3.5\text{ cm}$ radius bowl holds $\frac{2}{3}\pi (3.5)^3 \approx 89.8\text{ cm}^3$ of water.

14.6 Guesstimates

🎯 Real-World Mathematical Guesstimates

Page 141–143
🥟 Lallan's Golgappa Stall

Cylindrical container ($r = 10\text{ cm}, h = 50\text{ cm}$) has volume $V = 5000\pi \approx 15,708\text{ cm}^3$.

  • Student 1: Sphere $r = 1.5\text{ cm}$, 60% full of pani $\implies 8.5\text{ cm}^3/\text{serving} \implies \mathbf{\approx 1850\text{ golgappas}}$.
  • Student 2: Sphere $r = 1.75\text{ cm}$, 80% full of pani $\implies 18\text{ cm}^3/\text{serving} \implies \mathbf{\approx 870\text{ golgappas}}$.
  • Takeaway: Guesstimate answers vary with assumptions; rigorous mathematical reasoning is what matters.
🎾 Tennis Balls & Sphere Packing Gaps

Classroom: $30\text{ ft} \times 30\text{ ft} \times 15\text{ ft} \approx 3.78 \times 10^8\text{ cm}^3$. Tennis ball ($r = 3\text{ cm}$) volume $\approx 113\text{ cm}^3$.

  • Dividing volumes directly gives 3.3 million (unrealistic upper bound).
  • Sphere Packing Gaps: Spheres cannot pack without leaving air voids!
  • Grid arrangement ($150 \times 150 \times 75$) yields $\mathbf{\approx 1,687,500\text{ balls}}$.
  • Reason unit cubes are used for volume: Cubes tile space with 100% packing efficiency (zero voids).

Exercise Highlights

⭐ Key Worked Exemplars: Exercises 14.1 & 14.2

Page 126, 129
1. Three Meeting Faces

Areas of 3 adjacent faces meeting at a vertex are $6\text{ cm}^2, 15\text{ cm}^2, 10\text{ cm}^2$. Find volume.

$$lw = 6, \quad wh = 15, \quad lh = 10$$ Multiply all three: $$(lwh)^2 = 6 \times 15 \times 10 = 900$$ $$V = lwh = \sqrt{900} = \mathbf{30\text{ cm}^3}$$
2. Largest Cylinder in Cube

A cube of edge $r\text{ cm}$ has the largest possible right circular cylinder carved out of it.

Cylinder diameter $= r \implies \text{radius} = \frac{r}{2}$
Cylinder height $= r$
$$V = \pi \left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^2 (r) = \mathbf{\frac{\pi r^3}{4}\text{ cm}^3}$$
3. Invariant Cylinder Volume

Base radius increases by 10%. Height decreases by $x\%$. Volume is unchanged. Find $x$.

$$V_1 = \pi r^2 h = \pi (1.1r)^2 h'$$ $$h' = \frac{h}{1.21} \approx 0.8264 h$$ $$x\% = 1 - \frac{1}{1.21} = \frac{0.21}{1.21} \approx \mathbf{17.36\%}$$

⭐ Advanced Worked Exemplars: Exercises 14.3 to End

1. Crow & Marbles in Glass

Glass ($r = 4\text{ cm}$) water must rise from $16\text{ cm}$ to $20\text{ cm}$ ($\Delta h = 4\text{ cm}$). Marbles of $r = 1\text{ cm}$.

$$\text{Volume needed} = \pi (4)^2 (4) = 64\pi\text{ cm}^3$$ $$\text{Volume of marble} = \frac{4}{3}\pi (1)^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi$$ $$\text{Marbles} = \frac{64\pi}{(4/3)\pi} = \mathbf{48\text{ marbles}}$$
2. Equator String Puzzle

A string around Earth ($R$) is lengthened by $1\text{ m}$. How high ($h$) does it float uniformly above the surface?

$$2\pi(R + h) - 2\pi R = 1\text{ m}$$ $$2\pi h = 1 \implies h = \frac{1}{2\pi} \approx \mathbf{0.16\text{ m (16 cm)}}$$ Universal for any sphere (Earth, Moon, or volleyball)!
3. Sphere Circumscribed Cylinder

Show sphere volume is $\frac{2}{3}$ of the smallest enclosing cylinder.

$$\text{Cylinder: } R = r, \ H = 2r$$ $$V_{\text{cyl}} = \pi r^2 (2r) = 2\pi r^3$$ $$V_{\text{sph}} = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = \frac{2}{3}(2\pi r^3) = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3} V_{\text{cyl}}}$$

Chapter 14 Summary

📋 Master Formula & Concept Checklist

Page 147–148
✅ Prismatic & Revolution Solids
  • Cuboid: $\text{TSA} = 2(lw+wh+lh)$; $\text{Volume} = lwh$.
  • Cube ($a$): $\text{TSA} = 6a^2$; $\text{Volume} = a^3$.
  • Right Circular Cylinder: $$\text{CSA} = 2\pi rh, \quad \text{TSA} = 2\pi r(h+r), \quad V = \pi r^2 h$$

✅ Pyramidal, Spherical & Packing Principles
  • Pyramids: $V = \frac{1}{3} \times \text{Base Area} \times h$ (true for any flat base).
  • Sphere: $\text{Surface Area} = 4\pi r^2$; $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
  • Hemisphere: $\text{CSA} = 2\pi r^2$; $\text{TSA} = 3\pi r^2$; $V = \frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
  • Archimedes Theorem: $V_{\text{sph}} = \frac{2}{3} V_{\text{cyl}}$ of the circumscribed cylinder.
  • Packing Principle: Cubes tile space 100%; spheres leave inherent void gaps.
