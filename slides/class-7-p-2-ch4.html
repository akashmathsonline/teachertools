PAGE 68–69

The Master Rule: Dividing by 10, 100, 1000, ...

Shifting the decimal point leftward by counting zeros in the divisor
📏 Left-Shift Rule: To divide any number by $10, 100, 1000, \dots$, write the number and shift its decimal point to the left by as many places as there are zeros in the divisor. Add leading zeros if needed.
🪜 Three-Step Systematic Execution
  • Step 1: Place an explicit decimal at the end of whole numbers (e.g., $123 \implies 123.$).
  • Step 2: Count zeros in divisor ($10 \to 1\text{ zero}$, $1000 \to 3\text{ zeros}$).
  • Step 3: Shift decimal left by that count:
    • $123 \div 10 = \mathbf{12.3}$ ($1$ shift)
    • $24 \div 100 = \mathbf{0.24}$ ($2$ shifts)
    • $12 \div 1000 = \mathbf{0.012}$ ($3$ shifts, prepend $0$)
📊 Multi-Digit Demonstrations
  • $678 \div 1000 = \mathbf{0.678}$
  • $12345 \div 1000 = \mathbf{12.345}$
  • $847 \div 10000 = \mathbf{0.0847}$ ($4$ zeros $\implies 4$ shifts)
  • $23 \div 1000 = \mathbf{0.023}$ ($3$ zeros $\implies 3$ shifts)
$$n \div 10^k \iff \text{Shift decimal point } k \text{ places to the left}$$

SECTION 4.2 DECIMAL PLACES LAW
PAGE 70–71

The Master Rule for Decimal Multiplication

Summing decimal places of factors to position the point in the final product
$$\text{Total Decimal Places in Product} = (\text{Places in Multiplier}) + (\text{Places in Multiplicand})$$
🔍 Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Step 1: Ignore decimal points completely and multiply as counting whole numbers.
  • Step 2: Count the digits after the decimal point in both numbers.
  • Step 3: Place the decimal point in the product so that it has the sum of those decimal places, counting from the right!
  • Example: $5.96 \times 24.8$
    • $596 \times 248 = 147808$
    • Places: $2 + 1 = 3 \implies \mathbf{147.808}$
📝 Worked Example 5 ($5.8 \times 1.24$)
  • Whole number product: $$58 \times 124 = 7192$$
  • Counting decimal places:
    • $5.8 \implies 1\text{ decimal place}$
    • $1.24 \implies 2\text{ decimal places}$
    • Total places $= 1 + 2 = 3$
  • Count $3$ digits from right: $$\mathbf{5.8 \times 1.24 = 7.192}$$

SECTION 4.2 PRODUCT SIZE BEHAVIOUR
PAGE 72

Is the Product Always Greater than the Factors?

Analyzing how numbers smaller than 1 contract the size of their product
🧠 Unlike whole number multiplication (where $a \times b \ge a, b$), multiplying by a decimal less than $1$ scales down the quantity!
📈 Situation 1: Both $> 1$
  • Both factors $> 1$:
    • $3.4 \times 6.5 = \mathbf{22.1}$
    • $2.25 \times 8 = \mathbf{18}$
  • Result: Product is greater than both factors!
  • $$P > a \quad\text{and}\quad P > b$$
📉 Situation 2: Both in $(0, 1)$
  • Both factors between $0$ and $1$:
    • $0.75 \times 0.4 = \mathbf{0.3}$
    • $0.25 \times 0.8 = \mathbf{0.2}$
  • Result: Product is strictly less than both factors!
  • $$P < a \quad\text{and}\quad P < b$$
⚖️ Situation 3: Mixed
  • One factor in $(0, 1)$, one $> 1$:
    • $0.75 \times 5 = \mathbf{3.75}$
    • $0.25 \times 8 = \mathbf{2}$
  • Result: Product is between the two numbers:
  • $$0.75 < \mathbf{3.75} < 5$$

Division with Decimal Quotients: Place Value Regrouping

Extending long division seamlessly past the ones place into tenths and hundredths
🌟 The Place Value Transition: When remainder in the Ones place cannot be divided, regroup $1\text{ One} = 10\text{ Tenths}$, and place a decimal point in the quotient!
🪜 Deconstructing $1325 \div 4$ (Pages 78–80)
  • $1325 \div 4 = 331$ with remainder $1\text{ One}$.
  • Regroup 1 One into 10 Tenths:
    • $10\text{ Tenths} \div 4 = \mathbf{2\text{ Tenths}}$ with remainder $2\text{ Tenths}$.
    • Place decimal in quotient: $331.2\dots$
  • Regroup 2 Tenths into 20 Hundredths:
    • $20\text{ Hundredths} \div 4 = \mathbf{5\text{ Hundredths}}$, remainder $0$.
  • Final quotient $= \mathbf{331.25}$!
🪜 Deconstructing $237 \div 8$ (Pages 81–82)
  • $237 \div 8 = 29$ with remainder $5\text{ Ones}$.
  • $5\text{ Ones} = 50\text{ Tenths} \div 8 = \mathbf{6\text{ Tenths}}$, rem $2$.
  • $2\text{ Tenths} = 20\text{ Hundredths} \div 8 = \mathbf{2\text{ Hundredths}}$, rem $4$.
  • $4\text{ Hundredths} = 40\text{ Thousandths} \div 8 = \mathbf{5\text{ Thousandths}}$.
  • Final quotient $= \mathbf{29.625}$!

Handling Decimal Divisors: Transforming into Whole Numbers

Multiplying both dividend and divisor by equal powers of 10 to clear decimal divisors
🔄 The Golden Rule of Division: Multiply both dividend and divisor by $10, 100, 1000, \dots$ until the divisor becomes a whole number. The quotient remains strictly invariant!
🛵 Ravi's Scooter Trip ($126\text{ km} \div 2.5\text{ h}$)

  • Carry out long division: $$\frac{1260}{25} = \mathbf{50.4\text{ km/h}}$$
🔍 Example 13: $4.68 \div 1.3$ and $4.68 \div 0.13$

SECTION 4.3 NON-TERMINATING DECIMALS
PAGE 84–85

"Does This Ever End?" Non-Terminating Decimals

When remainders repeat forever in cyclic loops: Exploring $10 \div 3$ and $1 \div 7$
♾️ The Endless $10 \div 3$
  • $10 \div 3 = 3$ with remainder $1$.
  • Regroup $1\text{ One} = 10\text{ Tenths} \implies 10 \div 3 = 3$, rem $1$.
  • Regroup $1\text{ Tenth} = 10\text{ Hundredths} \implies 10 \div 3 = 3$, rem $1$.
  • At every step, the remainder is identically $1$!
  • The process never ends: $$10 \div 3 = 3.333333\dots$$
🔄 The 6-Digit Cycle of $1 \div 7$
  • Dividing $1$ by $7$: $$1 \div 7 = 0.142857142857\dots$$
  • Remainder Chain: $$1 \to 3 \to 2 \to 6 \to 4 \to 5 \to 1 \to \dots$$
  • Because a divisor of $7$ has only $6$ non-zero possible remainders $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, by the pigeonhole principle it must repeat!
💡 Core Theorem: Any rational fraction $p/q$ either terminates (if $q$ has only factors $2$ and $5$) or repeats in a periodic cycle of at most $q - 1$ digits!

The Magic Number $142857$ & Artin's Conjecture

A number whose multiples are cyclic permutations of itself, and an unsolved 1927 riddle
🌀 Cyclic Wheel Multiplications
  • $142857 \times 1 = \mathbf{142857}$
  • $142857 \times 2 = \mathbf{285714}$
  • $142857 \times 3 = \mathbf{428571}$
  • $142857 \times 4 = \mathbf{571428}$
  • $142857 \times 5 = \mathbf{714285}$
  • $142857 \times 6 = \mathbf{857142}$
  • Every product rotates the same 6 digits in circular order!
  • Multiply by $7$: $142857 \times 7 = \mathbf{999999}$!
🏛️ Emil Artin's Unsolved Conjecture (1927)
  • Other cyclic numbers arise from full-period primes (e.g., $1/17$ has a 16-digit cycle, $1/19$ has an 18-digit cycle).
  • The Big Question: Are there infinitely many such cyclic numbers?
  • In 1927, Austrian mathematician Emil Artin conjectured that there are infinitely many.
  • Nearly a century later, Artin's conjecture remains unproven!

$128 \div 0.4 = \mathbf{320}$ (greater than $128$!).

Under what condition on the divisor is the quotient strictly greater than the dividend?
Whenever the divisor is a positive number strictly less than 1 ($0 < d < 1$).

Dividend, Divisor, and Quotient Relationships

How dividing by decimals less than 1 magnifies the quotient

  • $$Q = \text{Dividend}$$
  • The neutral threshold point between shrinking and magnifying.

Section 4.4: Look Before You Leap! (The 365.2422 Day Year)

Why the Earth's true solar orbit requires mathematical calendar adjustments
🌍 The Solar Orbit Reality
  • Earth takes exactly $365.2422\text{ days}$ to complete one revolution around the Sun.
  • A standard calendar counts only $365$ days.
  • Each year, the calendar lags behind the Sun by: $$365.2422 - 365 = \mathbf{0.2422\text{ days}}$$
  • After 100 years: $$100 \times 0.2422 = \mathbf{24.22\text{ days}}$$
  • Without corrections, calendar months would drift out of sync with seasons!
📅 Adjustment 1: The 4-Year Rule
  • $0.2422 \approx \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$ day per year.
  • The Rule: Add $1$ extra day (February 29) every $4$th year ($366$ days).
  • The Math after 100 years:
    • Calendar days $= 100 \times 365 + 25 = \mathbf{36,525\text{ days}}$
    • Earth solar days $= 100 \times 365.2422 = \mathbf{36,524.22\text{ days}}$
  • Result: Calendar has overshot by $0.78$ days! We overcompensated!

Refining the Calendar: The 100-Year and 400-Year Rules

How Pope Gregory's astronomers balanced decimal astronomical fractions
⛔ Adjustment 2: Skip Century Years
  • The Rule: If divisible by $100$, it is NOT a leap year!
  • In $100$ years: only $24$ leap years ($76$ regular). $$\text{Days} = 24 \times 366 + 76 \times 365 = \mathbf{36,524\text{ days}}$$
  • Earth takes $36,524.22$ days $\implies$ difference is only $0.22$ days!
  • After 1000 years: Difference $= 10 \times 0.22 = \mathbf{2.2\text{ days}}$ (lagging behind).
🏛️ Adjustment 3: The 400-Year Rule
  • The Rule: If divisible by $400$, it IS a leap year!
  • Years $1600, 2000, 2400 \implies$ Leap Years!
  • Years $1700, 1800, 1900, 2100 \implies$ Not Leap Years!
  • In 1000 years: $$\text{Calendar Days} = \mathbf{3,65,242\text{ days}}$$ $$\text{Earth Solar Days} = \mathbf{3,65,242.2\text{ days}}$$
  • Difference is only $0.2$ days in 1000 years!
$$\text{Leap Year Criteria: Divisible by 4 } \xrightarrow{\text{except century}} \text{Divisible by 100? } \xrightarrow{\text{unless}} \text{Divisible by 400!}$$

Connecting consecutive integers across horizontal, vertical, and diagonal adjacency
