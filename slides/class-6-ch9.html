Section 9.1 Line of Symmetry & Mirror Halves
Pages 219–220
✂️ The Folding Test

Fold an isosceles triangle along its central dotted line: one half covers the other half completely and perfectly.

These overlapping halves are called Mirror Halves.

A line that cuts a figure into two parts that exactly overlap upon folding is a Line of Symmetry (or Axis of Symmetry).

🧩 Non-Examples (Puzzle Pieces)

Consider a 4-piece jigsaw puzzle with tabs and blanks:

  • A dotted line passes through the middle.
  • When folded, the interlocking tabs and hollows do not align.
  • The halves do not overlap.
  • Therefore, that dotted line is not a line of symmetry!

Folding Analysis Multiple Lines: Square vs. Rectangle
Pages 220–221
🟦 The 4 Folds of a Square
  • Fold 1: Vertical fold (left edge onto right edge).
  • Fold 2: Horizontal fold (top edge onto bottom edge).
  • Fold 3: Diagonal fold (top-left to bottom-right).
  • Fold 4: Second diagonal fold (top-right to bottom-left).
A Square has exactly 4 Lines of Symmetry!
📏 The Rectangle Mystery: Diagonal?

Does a non-square rectangle have a diagonal line of symmetry?

Observation: When folded along its diagonal, the corners stick out! The two halves have equal area, but they do not overlap!

• A non-square rectangle has ONLY 2 lines of symmetry (horizontal & vertical midlines).
• Its diagonals are NOT lines of symmetry!

Geometry Insight Reflection Symmetry & Point Mapping
Pages 221–222
🪞 The Mirror Line Concept

Folding along a line of symmetry is identical to placing a plane mirror perpendicular to the paper. Every point on one side maps directly to a matching point on the opposite side at the same perpendicular distance!

Vertical Reflection of Square ABCD

Corners: Top-left $A$, Top-right $B$, Bottom-right $C$, Bottom-left $D$.
Reflect across the vertical midline:

$$B \longleftrightarrow A \quad\text{and}\quad C \longleftrightarrow D$$

Point $B$ goes to position $A$, and point $C$ goes to position $D$!

Diagonal Reflection Across AC

Reflect across diagonal line segment $AC$:

$$A \longrightarrow A, \quad C \longrightarrow C, \quad B \longleftrightarrow D$$

Points $A$ and $C$ lie directly on the mirror line, so they remain fixed, while $B$ and $D$ swap positions!

🎨 Ink Blot Devils

1. Fold a blank sheet in half.
2. Spill ink drops on one half.
3. Press firmly and open out.
Result: An intricate, organic symmetric shape! The central crease is the exact line of symmetry.

✂️ Single-Fold Cuts

Fold paper once along a line. Cut any design out of the doubled edge. When unfolded, the cut boundary duplicates symmetrically on both sides of the fold line.

🏮 Multi-Fold Paper Lace

Fold a thin strip multiple times (accordion or grid). Snip geometric notches along the folded edges to generate repeating festoons, borders, and decorative bunting for festivals!

🎯 The Core Rule of Fold-Generation

Every fold line acts as a potential mirror line. $N$ folds through the centre multiply single incisions into $2^N$ or $2N$ symmetrically repeating features!

Classroom Activity The Punching Game: Identifying Fold Lines
Pages 223–224
📌 How Hole Punches Reveal Crease Lines

When a folded sheet is punched with a single hole and unfolded, the resulting holes lie symmetrically on opposite sides of the crease. The crease is the perpendicular bisector connecting paired holes!

Case 1: Vertical Holes

Two holes aligned horizontally near the side edges indicate a vertical fold line down the centre.

Case 2: Diagonal Holes

Two holes near opposite corners symmetric to a diagonal indicate the paper was folded along that diagonal line.

Case 3: Four Corner Holes

A single punch creating four holes at all 4 corners requires two folds: one vertical followed by one horizontal!

Theorem & Pattern Symmetry in Regular Polygons

👑 The Regular Polygon Symmetry Rule

A polygon is regular if all its sides are equal and all its interior angles are equal.

A regular polygon with $n$ sides has exactly $n$ lines of symmetry!
Equilateral Triangle 3 equal sides
3 Lines
Vertex to midpoint
Square 4 equal sides
4 Lines
2 midlines + 2 diagonals
Regular Pentagon 5 equal sides
5 Lines
Vertex to opp. midpoint
Regular Hexagon 6 equal sides
6 Lines
3 diagonals + 3 midlines
Sequence formed by lines of symmetry: $3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, \dots, n$ (The natural numbers starting from 3!).

Section 9.2 Rotational Symmetry: The Paper Windmill
Pages 230–231
🌀 The Windmill Paradox

Look at a 4-blade paper pinwheel:

  • Try folding it across any line: no two halves overlap. It has zero lines of symmetry!
  • Now rotate it about its central pivot pin by $90^\circ$: it fits its own outline perfectly!
  • It repeats at $180^\circ$, $270^\circ$, and $360^\circ$.
This property is called Rotational Symmetry!
🎯 Key Technical Definitions

• Centre of Rotation: The fixed point about which the figure turns (e.g., the pin in the windmill).

• Angle of Symmetry: An angle through which a figure is turned to look identical to its initial state.

• Full Turn Fact: Rotating by $360^\circ$ always brings every object back to itself. Hence, a figure is said to have rotational symmetry only if it has an angle of symmetry strictly between $0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$!

Radial Design Radial Arms: Determining Angles of Rotation
Pages 232–234
4 Radial Arms (Cross)

• Angle between arms: $\frac{360^\circ}{4} = 90^\circ$
• Angles of symmetry:
$90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ, 360^\circ$
• Total: 4 angles of symmetry.

3 Radial Arms (Tri-Star)

• Must distribute $360^\circ$ equally: $\angle A = \angle B = \angle C = \frac{360^\circ}{3} = 120^\circ$
• Angles of symmetry:
$120^\circ, 240^\circ, 360^\circ$
• If angles are unequal, rotational symmetry vanishes!

2 Radial Arms (Z / S Shape)

• Angle between opposite arms: $180^\circ$
• Angles of symmetry:
$180^\circ, 360^\circ$
• Order of symmetry = 2.

📐 Fundamental Radial Theorem: For a figure with $n$ equally spaced identical radial arms, the smallest angle of symmetry is $\frac{360^\circ}{n}$, and all other angles of symmetry are exact integer multiples of this smallest angle!

Mathematical Laws Order of Symmetry & The Divisor Rule
Pages 236–237
🔢 Order of Rotational Symmetry

The number of times a figure fits onto itself in one full revolution ($360^\circ$) is called its order of rotational symmetry.

• Order 2 $\implies$ Angles: $180^\circ, 360^\circ$
• Order 3 $\implies$ Angles: $120^\circ, 240^\circ, 360^\circ$
• Order 4 $\implies$ Angles: $90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ, 360^\circ$
• Order 6 $\implies$ Angles: $60^\circ, 120^\circ, 180^\circ, 240^\circ, 300^\circ, 360^\circ$
⚖️ The Divisor Principle (True / False)

• Law 1: Every figure has $360^\circ$ as an angle of symmetry. (True)

• Law 2: If the smallest angle of symmetry is a whole number of degrees, it MUST be a factor of $360$! (True)

Application: Can a figure have $45^\circ$ as its smallest angle? Yes, because $360 \div 45 = 8$.
Can it have $17^\circ$? No, because 17 does not divide 360!

Infinite Symmetry The Circle & Real-World Rotations
Pages 237–238
⚪ The Infinite Symmetry of a Circle

The circle is the most symmetrical geometric shape:

  • Reflection: Every diameter passing through the centre is a line of symmetry $\implies$ infinitely many lines of symmetry!
  • Rotation: Rotate by any angle whatsoever (e.g., $1^\circ, 0.5^\circ, 33.7^\circ$) about the centre, and the circle coincides with itself $\implies$ infinitely many angles of symmetry!
🚲 Real-World Rotational Objects

• Ceiling Fan (3 blades): Rotational order 3 ($120^\circ, 240^\circ, 360^\circ$).
• Bicycle Wheel (spoked): Order depends on the number of spokes.
• Ashoka Chakra (24 spokes): Exactly 24 lines of reflection symmetry and 24 angles of rotational symmetry (smallest turn $= \frac{360^\circ}{24} = 15^\circ$)!

Worked Examples Exercise Highlights & Solutions
Pages 228–238
Problem 1: Triangles

Question: Can a triangle have exactly two lines of symmetry?

Analysis:
• Scalene: 0 lines
• Isosceles: Exactly 1 line
• Equilateral: Exactly 3 lines

Answer: Impossible! A triangle has 0, 1, or 3 lines of symmetry, never 2.

Problem 2: Quadrilaterals

Question: Name a quadrilateral with rotational symmetry but no reflection symmetry.

Analysis:
A non-rectangular parallelogram has order 2 rotational symmetry ($180^\circ, 360^\circ$) but 0 lines of symmetry!

Answer: Parallelogram.

Problem 3: Missing Turns

Question: A figure has $60^\circ$ as an angle of symmetry and two smaller angles. Find its smallest angle.

Analysis:
If $60^\circ$ is the 3rd multiple of the smallest angle $\theta$:
$3\theta = 60^\circ \implies \theta = 20^\circ$.

Answer: $20^\circ$ (Angles: $20^\circ, 40^\circ, 60^\circ$).

Chapter Summary Core Takeaways & Rules Checklist
Pages 241
✅ Reflection Symmetry Checklist
  • Line of Symmetry: The crease along which a folded shape overlaps exactly into mirror halves.
  • Reflection Property: Every point is mapped perpendicular to the mirror line at equal distance.
  • Regular Polygons: An $n$-sided regular polygon has exactly $n$ lines of symmetry.
  • Rectangle vs. Square: Square has 4 lines; rectangle has 2; rectangle diagonals are NOT lines of symmetry.
✅ Rotational Symmetry Checklist
  • Centre of Rotation: The fixed anchor point about which rotation occurs.
  • Angles of Symmetry: Angles between $0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$ that restore the original outline.
  • Divisor Law: If the smallest angle is a whole number of degrees, it must divide $360^\circ$ cleanly.
  • Independent Symmetries: A shape may have lines but no rotational symmetry, rotations but no lines, or both!
