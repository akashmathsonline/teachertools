Propositions and Converses Defined

The structure of mathematical assertions and direction of implication

1
What is a Proposition?

A statement that is definitely either true or false is called a proposition.

Original Proposition (If $X$ then $Y$):

"If two sides of a triangle are equal, then the angles opposite the equal sides are equal."

$X \implies Y$ : "Condition $X$ implies Result $Y$".

2
What is its Converse?

Swapping the condition and consequence creates the converse ($Y \implies X$).

Converse Proposition (If $Y$ then $X$):

"If two angles of a triangle are equal, then the sides opposite the equal angles have equal lengths."

Core Question: If a proposition is true, is its converse always true?

Counterexamples: Disproving False Propositions

A single contradictory instance is sufficient to shatter a general claim

Example 1: The Wet Road
  • Proposition $P$: If it rains, then the road is wet. (True)
  • Converse $Q$: If the road is wet, then it has rained. (Not necessarily true!)
  • Counterexample: A municipal water tanker spilled water.
  • Takeaway: Finding one valid counterexample proves the converse is false.
Euler Disproves Fermat's Prime Claim
  • Pierre de Fermat conjectured:
    $$F_n = 2^{2^n} + 1 \quad \text{are prime for all } n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$
  • Verified: $F_0=3, F_1=5, F_2=17, F_3=257, F_4=65537$ (all prime).
  • Euler's Counterexample ($n=5$):
    $$2^{2^5} + 1 = 2^{32} + 1 = 641 \times 6{,}700{,}417$$

Divisibility Implication: Multiples of 6 vs 3

When one direction holds, but the converse breaks

Proposition $P$ (True)

"If a number is a multiple of 6, then it is a multiple of 3."

  • Let $n = 6k = 3(2k)$, where $k$ is an integer.
  • Since $2k$ is an integer, $n$ is divisible by 3.
Converse $Q$ (False)

"If a number is a multiple of 3, then it is a multiple of 6."

  • Counterexamples:
    $n = 3, \quad n = 9, \quad n = 15$
  • They are multiples of 3, but not multiples of 6!

Perfect Squares and Odd Number of Factors

When both proposition and converse are mutually true!

The Factor-Partner Principle
  • Every factor of $n$ has a partner factor $g$ such that $f \times g = n$.
  • For $12$: $(1, 12), (2, 6), (3, 4) \implies 6$ factors (even).
  • For $36$: $(1, 36), (2, 18), (3, 12), (4, 9), (6, 6) \implies 9$ factors (odd).
Bi-directional Implication ($P \iff Q$)
  • Proposition $P$: If $n$ is a perfect square, then $n$ has an odd number of factors. (True)
  • Converse $Q$: If $n$ has an odd number of factors, then $n$ is a perfect square. (True)
  • Conclusion: 'Perfect square' and 'odd number of factors' imply each other!

Area and Congruence: One-Way Implication

Examining shape superimposition versus numerical region measure

Proposition $P$ (False)

"If two triangles have the same area, then they are congruent."

  • Counterexample:
    • $\triangle T_1$: base $4\text{ cm}$, height $3\text{ cm} \implies \text{Area} = 6\text{ cm}^2$
    • $\triangle T_2$: base $12\text{ cm}$, height $1\text{ cm} \implies \text{Area} = 6\text{ cm}^2$
  • Different shapes, same area!
Converse $Q$ (True)

"If two triangles are congruent, then they have the same area."

  • Congruent triangles can be exactly superimposed.
  • They enclose the exact same region of space.

The Baudhāyana-Pythagoras Theorem & Converse

Rigorous proof of the converse using auxiliary right triangle and SSS

Theorem & its Converse Statement
Baudhāyana-Pythagoras Theorem:

If $\angle C = 90^\circ$, then $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

Converse Statement:

If $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, then the triangle is right-angled ($\angle C = 90^\circ$).

Proof of the Converse
  • Given: $\triangle ABC$ satisfying $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.
  • Construction: Construct $\triangle XYZ$ with $\angle Z = 90^\circ$, legs $YZ = a$ and $XZ = b$.
  • In $\triangle XYZ$: $XY^2 = a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \implies XY = c$.
  • By SSS Congruence, $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle XYZ \implies \angle C = \angle Z = 90^\circ$!

Expressing Implication in Different Ways

Recognizing logical equivalence across mathematical prose

Equivalent Formulations of $X \implies Y$
  • Standard conditional: "If $X$, then $Y$."
  • Direct implication: "$X$ implies $Y$."
  • Reversed order: "$Y$, when $X$." OR "$Y$ if $X$."
Real-Life vs Mathematical Conditionals
  • Everyday language is often sloppy: People mistakenly assume the converse is implied too.
  • In mathematics, precision is sacred: The converse must be treated as a brand-new proposition demanding its own independent proof.

Counterexamples in Prime Generation Claims

There are no simple neat algebraic formulas that generate only primes

Claim: $4n^2 + 1$ is Prime
  • $n=1 \implies 5$ (Prime)
  • $n=2 \implies 17$ (Prime)
  • Counterexample ($n=4$):
    $$4(4^2)+1 = 65 = 5 \times 13$$
Claim: $n^2 + n + 11$ is Prime
  • $n=1 \implies 13$ (Prime)
  • $n=2 \implies 17$ (Prime)
  • Counterexample ($n=11$):
    $$11(11+1+1) = 11 \times 13$$
Claim: $4^n + 3$ is Prime
  • $n=1 \implies 7$ (Prime)
  • $n=2 \implies 19$ (Prime)
  • Counterexample ($n=4$):
    $$4^4+3 = 259 = 7 \times 37$$

The Two-Stick Quadrilateral Challenge

Analyzing how converses dictate construction requirements

Scenario (i): Property of Class Q

"If a quadrilateral is of type Q, then it has equal-length diagonals."

  • Question (a): To build type Q, must sticks be equal?
    • Yes! Otherwise it cannot be type Q.
  • Question (b): Does crossing matter?
    • Yes! Crossing at midpoints vs non-midpoints produces different shapes.
Scenario (ii): The Converse Condition

"If a quadrilateral has equal diagonals, then it is of type Q."

  • Question (a): Should sticks be equal?
    • Yes! This triggers the sufficient condition.
  • Question (b): Does crossing matter?
    • No! ANY arrangement qualifies as type Q.

Exercise Highlights — Step-by-Step Analysis

Representative problems testing converse formulation and truth values

1. Real Numbers ($x^2 = y^2$)

Prop: If $x = y$, then $x^2 = y^2$. (True)

Converse: If $x^2 = y^2$, then $x = y$.

Counterexample: $x = 3, y = -3$. Then $3^2 = (-3)^2 = 9$, but $3 \neq -3$.

Converse is False
2. Primes & 3 Factors

Prop: If $n = p^2$ (prime $p$), then $n$ has exactly 3 factors.

Proof: Factors of $p^2$ are $\{1, p, p^2\}$. Total = 3. (True)

Both Prop & Converse True
3. Divisibility by 4 & 6

Prop: If $n$ is divisible by 24, then $n$ is divisible by 4 and 6. (True)

Counterexample: $n = 12$. $12$ is divisible by 4 and 6, but NOT by 24!

Converse is False

Chapter Summary: Logical Toolkit

Master recap of propositions, converses, and counterexamples

Foundations of Mathematical Logic (✅)
  • Proposition: A declarative statement strictly True or False.
  • Form of Implication: "If $X$ then $Y$" is written as $X \implies Y$.
  • Converse Formation: Formed by reversing condition and result ($Y \implies X$).
  • Independent Truth: Truth of proposition does NOT guarantee truth of converse.
Four Truth-Value Possibilities (✅)
  • Prop True, Converse False: Multiple of 6 implies multiple of 3; $x=y \implies x^2=y^2$.
  • Both True: Perfect square $\iff$ odd number of factors.
  • Both False: Statements where both directions fail.
  • Counterexample Power: A single valid counterexample disproves an entire claim.
