7.1 • RECTANGLES & SQUARES Pages 148–149

Equi-Area Partitions & The Unit Square Measure

🧩 Creative Equal-Area Partitions

  • Infinitely Many Divisions: A square can be divided into 4 parts of equal area in endless creative shapes.
  • Equal Compression & Expansion: If a boundary line is modified by pushing inwards and pulling outwards by identical amounts, the total enclosed area remains invariant.
  • Rangoli Context: Comparing surface coverage depends entirely on how much space a boundary covers, not merely its boundary outline.

🟩 Counting Unit Squares

Area is formally measured by counting the number of non-overlapping unit squares ($1\text{ cm} \times 1\text{ cm}$) packed inside:

  • $7\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm}$ rectangle contains $7 \times 4 = 28$ unit squares ($28\text{ cm}^2$).
  • $8\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm}$ rectangle contains $8 \times 3 = 24$ unit squares ($24\text{ cm}^2$).
$$\text{Area of a Rectangle} = \text{length} \times \text{width}$$

7.1 • CONCEPT COMPARISON Page 150

Why Perimeter Cannot Measure Area

⚠️ Perimeter Independence

  • Equal Perimeter $\neq$ Equal Area: Regions can share identical perimeter but have completely different areas.
  • Counter-Intuitive Reversal: A region with a strictly larger perimeter can actually enclose a smaller area! $$\text{Perimeter}(\text{Region 1}) > \text{Perimeter}(\text{Region 2})$$ $$\text{yet } \text{Area}(\text{Region 1}) < \text{Area}(\text{Region 2})$$
  • Boundary length only measures the linear path around the perimeter; area measures 2D space inside.

🔍 Diagonal Split of a Rectangle

The diagonal divides any rectangle into two congruent right triangles:

  • Each triangle covers exactly half the unit squares of the outer rectangle.
  • For dimensions $7\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm}$: $$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times 7 \times 4 = 14\text{ cm}^2$$
Key Takeaway: Two shapes with equal perimeter do NOT necessarily hold equal internal surface capacity!

7.1 • PATHS & COMPOSITE REGIONS Pages 151–152

Surrounding Paths, Cross-Paths, and Tube Dissections

🛣️ Outer Paths & Shifting Invariance

  • Outer Path Formula: $$\text{Area}(\text{Path}) = \text{Area}(\text{Outer } ABCD) - \text{Area}(\text{Inner } EFGH)$$
  • Invariance under Movement: As long as the inner rectangular park $EFGH$ remains entirely inside $ABCD$, the total area of the path does NOT change when the boundary shifts!
  • Cross-Paths: Computed by summing both rectangular paths and subtracting their overlapping square intersection once.

🌀 Spiral Tube Unfolding

  • Constant Width Principle: A complex folded tube or spiral of uniform width $w$ can be decomposed into rectilinear segments.
  • Equivalent Straight Tube: A bent tube of equal area is equivalent to a single uncoiled straight tube of equal midline length and width.
  • Scaling Effect: When the side length of a square doubles, its total enclosed area increases by a factor of $2^2 = 4$.

7.2 • AREA OF A TRIANGLE Pages 153–154

Area of a Triangle: Acute, Right, and Obtuse

📐 Rectangular Enclosure Proof

Enclose $\triangle ABC$ inside rectangle $BCDE$ with base $BC$ and height $h$:

  • The altitude $AX$ splits the triangle into two right triangles.
  • Each right triangle has half the area of its corresponding sub-rectangle.
  • Summing both halves gives: $$\text{Area}(\triangle ABC) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$$

🔺 Obtuse Triangle Derivation

When the altitude falls outside the base ($D$ lies on extended $CB$):

$$\text{Area}(\triangle ABC) = \text{Area}(\triangle ADC) - \text{Area}(\triangle ADB)$$
  • $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}h(DC) - \frac{1}{2}h(DB) = \frac{1}{2}h(DC - DB)$
  • Since $DC - DB = BC$, we obtain: $$\mathbf{\text{Area}(\triangle ABC) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}}$$
The formula holds universal validity across every triangle classification!

7.2 • THEOREMS & APPLICATIONS Pages 155–156

Double-Altitude Computation & The Median Theorem

📏 Finding Unknown Altitudes

Equating two different base-altitude views of the same triangle:

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times b_1 \times h_1 = \frac{1}{2} \times b_2 \times h_2$$
  • Given $BC = 5$, altitude $AX = 3 \implies \text{Area} = \frac{15}{2}$.
  • With side $AC = 4$ and unknown altitude $BY$: $$\frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times BY = \frac{15}{2} \implies 2BY = 7.5 \implies BY = 3.75$$

⚖️ Median Area Bisector Theorem

Theorem: The line joining a vertex of a triangle to the midpoint of its opposite side (median) divides the triangle into two triangles of equal area.
  • Both sub-triangles share the exact same altitude dropped from the common vertex.
  • Their bases are identical in length ($OD = OB$).
  • Hence, diagonals of a rectangle bisect it into 4 triangles of equal area!

7.2 • OPTIMIZATION & REFLECTION Pages 156–157

Triangles Between Parallel Lines & Minimum Perimeter

📐 Constant Area Property ($l \parallel BC$)

  • Consider base $BC$ fixed, and third vertex $A$ moving anywhere along line $l \parallel BC$.
  • Since distance between parallel lines is constant, altitude $h$ never changes.
  • Result: ALL triangles on base $BC$ with vertex on $l$ have the exact same area!
$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times BC \times h = \text{Constant}$$

🪞 Optical Reflection for Shortest Path

  • To minimize perimeter $BC + AB + AC$, we must minimize $AB + AC$.
  • Reflect $C$ across mirror line $l$ to $C'$. Then $AC = AC'$.
  • The path $B \rightarrow A \rightarrow C$ equals $B \rightarrow A \rightarrow C'$.
  • Shortest distance between two points is a straight line: connect $B$ directly to $C'$!
  • $A$ must lie on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$ (Isosceles triangle).

7.2 • TRIANGULATION Pages 159–160

Triangulation: Computing the Area of Any Polygon

🔺 Fundamental Triangulation Principle

  • Every polygon (quadrilateral, pentagon, hexagon, etc.) can be completely divided into non-overlapping triangles.
  • Once triangulated, the total area of the polygon is simply the sum of the areas of its component triangles: $$\text{Area}(\text{Polygon}) = \sum \text{Area}(\triangle_i)$$
  • Therefore, knowing how to compute triangle areas unlocks the area of ANY polygon!

📐 General Quadrilateral Formula

For quadrilateral $ABCD$ with diagonal $AC$ and perpendicular offsets $h_1, h_2$:

$$\text{Area}(ABCD) = \frac{1}{2} \times AC \times (h_1 + h_2)$$
  • $\text{Area}(\triangle ABC) = \frac{1}{2} \times AC \times h_1$
  • $\text{Area}(\triangle ADC) = \frac{1}{2} \times AC \times h_2$
  • Summing gives half the product of diagonal and sum of perpendicular offsets!

7.2 • PARALLELOGRAMS Pages 161–163

Area of a Parallelogram: Dissection to Rectangle

✂️ Dissection by RHS Congruence

  • Drop perpendicular $AX \perp CD$ (height $h$).
  • Cut right triangle $\triangle AXD$ and shift it to the other end.
  • By RHS congruence, $\triangle AXD \cong \triangle BYC$ because: $$\angle AXD = \angle BYC = 90^\circ, \; AX = BY, \; AD = BC$$
  • The pieces fit seamlessly into rectangle $ABYX$!

📐 The Master Formula

Since $DX = CY$, the base of the rectangle $XY = DC = \text{base}$:

$$\text{Area of a Parallelogram} = \text{base} \times \text{height}$$
  • Any side can serve as base, matched with its own perpendicular distance to the opposite side.
  • Equal Sides Comparison: A rectangle and a non-rectangular parallelogram sharing identical side lengths $5\text{ cm}$ and $4\text{ cm}$ do NOT have equal area—the rectangle is strictly larger!

7.2 • RHOMBUS Pages 164–165

Area of a Rhombus: Diagonal Product Formula

💎 Perpendicular Diagonals Property

  • A rhombus is a parallelogram whose 4 sides are equal.
  • Its diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other.
  • The diagonals partition the rhombus into 4 congruent right triangles: $$\triangle AOB \cong \triangle COB \cong \triangle COD \cong \triangle AOD$$
  • Each right triangle has legs $\frac{d_1}{2}$ and $\frac{d_2}{2}$.

🧮 Two Derivations of the Formula

  • By Dissection (Śulba-Sūtras): Join halves into rectangle $WXYZ$ of dimensions $d_1$ by $\frac{d_2}{2}$. $$\text{Area} = d_1 \times \frac{d_2}{2} = \frac{1}{2} d_1 d_2$$
  • By Triangle Sum: $\triangle ADB + \triangle CDB$: $$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(AO \cdot BD) + \frac{1}{2}(CO \cdot BD) = \frac{1}{2}(AO + CO)BD = \frac{1}{2} d_1 d_2$$
$$\text{Area of a Rhombus} = \frac{1}{2} \times d_1 \times d_2$$

7.2 • TRAPEZIUM Pages 166–168

Area of a Trapezium: Two Elegant Derivations

Approach 1: Rectangle + Triangles

Drop perpendiculars $WM, XN$ creating sub-segments $x, a, y$ with $b = x+a+y$:

  • $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}hx + ha + \frac{1}{2}hy = \frac{1}{2}h(x + y + 2a)$
  • Substitute $x+y = b-a$: $$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}h(b - a + 2a) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}h(a + b)}$$

Approach 2: Joining Two Copies

Take two identical copies of trapezium with parallel sides $a$ and $b$, invert the second copy, and join along the non-parallel side:

  • Angles add to $x+y = 180^\circ \implies$ forms a single giant parallelogram!
  • Base of parallelogram $= a + b$, height $= h$.
  • Since it contains two identical trapeziums: $$\mathbf{\text{Area}(\text{Trapezium}) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{height} \times (a + b)}$$

7.3 • REAL LIFE MEASUREMENTS Pages 170–171

Areas in Everyday Life: Imperial Units & Land Measures

📐 Imperial to Metric Conversions

  • Linear Conversions: $1\text{ in} = 2.54\text{ cm}$, $1\text{ ft} = 12\text{ in}$.
  • Square Inch: $$1\text{ in}^2 = 2.54^2\text{ cm}^2 = 6.4516\text{ cm}^2$$
  • Square Foot: $$1\text{ ft}^2 = 12^2\text{ in}^2 = 144\text{ in}^2 \approx 929.03\text{ cm}^2$$
  • A4 Paper Size: $21\text{ cm} \times 29.7\text{ cm} = 623.7\text{ cm}^2$.

🌾 Large Land Units Across India

  • Acre: $1\text{ acre} = 43,560\text{ ft}^2 \approx 4046.86\text{ m}^2$.
  • Square Kilometer: $1\text{ km}^2 = 1,000,000\text{ m}^2$.
  • Regional Indian Units: Bigha, Gaj, Katha, Dhur, Cent, Guntha, Ankanam.
  • These varied regional units reflect India's rich, historic traditions of agricultural measurement.

EXERCISE HIGHLIGHTS Pages 157, 163, 169

Representative Worked Exercise Solutions

🔺 Dual Altitude (P. 158 Q2)

Given: Base $BC = 6$, $AX = 4$, side $AC = 8$. Find altitude $BY$.

$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 4 = 12$
Also $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times BY$
$4 \cdot BY = 12 \implies BY = 3$
Ans: $BY = 3\text{ units}$

▱ Parallelogram Heights (P. 163 Q3)

Given: Base $SR = 12$, $QM = 6$, side $PS = 7.6$. Find $QN$.

$\text{Area} = 12 \times 6 = 72\text{ cm}^2$
Also $\text{Area} = PS \times QN$
$7.6 \times QN = 72$
$QN = \frac{72}{7.6} \approx 9.47\text{ cm}$
Ans: $QN \approx 9.47\text{ cm}$

⏢ Trapezium Area (P. 169 Q3-ii)

Given: $a = 24\text{ m}$, $b = 36\text{ m}$, height $h = 14\text{ m}$.

$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} h (a + b)$
$= \frac{1}{2} \times 14 \times (24 + 36)$
$= 7 \times 60 = 420\text{ m}^2$
Ans: $\text{Area} = 420\text{ m}^2$

Chapter 7 Summary Checklist

✅ Core Principles & Formulas

  • Unit Square Measure: Area counts 2D space inside, independent of perimeter.
  • Triangle Formula: $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$ applies universally across acute, right, and obtuse types.

  • Trapezium: $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times h \times (a + b)$ (half height times sum of parallel sides).
  • Real-Life Measures: $1\text{ in}^2 = 6.4516\text{ cm}^2$, $1\text{ acre} = 43,560\text{ ft}^2$.
