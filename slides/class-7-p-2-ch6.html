Symmetry & The 'Eyes' Construction

Slide 2 / 28
👁️ The 'Eyes' Construction
  • In Grade 6, we drew symmetrical eyes using spatial estimation to pick centres $A$ (for the lower arc) and $B$ (for the upper arc).
  • The arcs intersect at endpoints $X$ and $Y$, defining a supporting line $XY$ which serves as the axis of symmetry.
  • For exact symmetry, the upper and lower arcs must have the identical radius:
    $$AX = AY = BX = BY$$
  • The supporting line $XY$ guides the accurate placement of centres $A$ and $B$.
📐 Geometric Setup

Drawing equal arcs from endpoints $X$ and $Y$ above and below $XY$ gives exact centres $A$ and $B$.

Perpendicular Bisector

Perpendicular Bisector & Congruence Proof

Slide 4 / 28
✨ Definitions & Observations
  • Bisection: Dividing a line segment or geometric object into two identical parts.
  • Perpendicular Bisector: A line that bisects a segment at $90^\circ$.
  • When $A$ and $B$ are joined, $AB$ intersects $XY$ at point $O$.
  • $O$ is the midpoint of $XY$ ($OX = OY$) and $AB \perp XY$.
⚖️ Proof via Congruence
  • In $\triangle ABX$ and $\triangle ABY$: $AX = AY$, $BX = BY$, and $AB$ is common. Thus, by SSS Congruence: $$\triangle ABX \cong \triangle ABY \implies \angle XAO = \angle YAO$$
  • In $\triangle AOX$ and $\triangle AOY$: $AX = AY$, $\angle XAO = \angle YAO$, and side $AO$ is common. By SAS Congruence: $$\triangle AOX \cong \triangle AOY$$
  • Hence, $OX = OY$ and $\angle AOX = \angle AOY = 90^\circ$ (linear pair sums to $180^\circ$).

Fundamental Locus

Varying Eye Shapes & The Fundamental Locus

Slide 6 / 28
🎨 Different Eye Expressions
  • Human eyes, owl eyes, cat eyes, and folk-art eyes (e.g. Kathakali, Jamini Roy style) have different proportions.
  • By choosing points $C$ and $D$ further away or closer, we obtain different arc curvatures for wide or narrow eyes.
  • As long as $CX = CY$ and $DX = DY$, the eye remains perfectly symmetrical.
🔑 The Fundamental Theorem
"Any point that is equidistant from endpoints $X$ and $Y$ MUST lie on the perpendicular bisector of $XY$."
  • Since a segment has only one unique perpendicular bisector, all such equidistant pairs ($A, B, C, D\dots$) lie on the same straight line $AB$.
  • This turns the perpendicular bisector into a line of centres for countless creative designs.

Construction

Constructing a Perpendicular Bisector

Slide 8 / 28
🧭 Step-by-Step Algorithm
  • Given: A line segment $XY$.
  • Step 1: With centre $X$ and fixed compass radius $r > \frac{1}{2}XY$, draw arcs on both sides of $XY$.
  • Step 2: With centre $Y$ and the exact same radius, draw intersecting arcs to locate points $A$ (above) and $B$ (below).
  • Step 3: Join $A$ and $B$ using an unmarked ruler. Line $AB$ is the required perpendicular bisector.
💡 "Figure it Out" Explorations
  • Different radii above and below? Yes! The radius for arc $A$ can differ from arc $B$, as long as each pair uses equal distance from $X$ and $Y$.
  • Arcs on the same side? Yes! Two points $A$ and $C$ on the same side of $XY$ (with different radii) still uniquely define the line $AC$.
  • Why radius $> \frac{1}{2}XY$? If radius is smaller, the arcs will never meet!

90° Angle

Constructing a $90^\circ$ Angle at a Given Point

Slide 10 / 28
📐 The Underlying Strategy
  • To construct a perpendicular at a point $O$ on line $m$, we turn $O$ into the midpoint of an auxiliary segment $XY$.
  • With centre $O$ and any convenient radius, swing arcs cutting the line on both sides at $X$ and $Y$ so that $OX = OY$.
  • Now, construct the perpendicular bisector of this created segment $XY$.
  • Since $O$ is already known to be on the bisector, we need only one point of intersection ($A$) above the line!
🎯 Construction Steps
  • Mark point $O$ on given line $m$.
  • Swing arcs with centre $O$ to mark points $X$ and $Y$ on line $m$.
  • With radius $> OX$, swing arcs from $X$ and $Y$ intersecting at $A$.
  • Draw ray $OA$. Then $\angle AOX = \angle AOY = 90^\circ$.
$$OA \perp m \quad \text{at point } O$$

Śulba-Sūtras

Vedic Geometry: The Śulba-Sūtras

Slide 12 / 28
📜 The Śulba-Sūtra Tradition
  • The Śulba-Sūtras (Baudhāyana, Āpastamba, Kātyāyana, Mānava) belong to the Kalpa Vedānga.
  • The Sanskrit word śulba literally means a cord or rope.
  • Ancient Indian mathematicians used a rope with pegs as a versatile tool: it served both as a straightedge (when taut) and as a compass (to swing arcs on the ground).
🪢 Kātyāyana-Śulbasūtra Method
  • Drive pegs into the ground at the endpoints $X$ and $Y$.
  • Take a long rope, form loops at both ends, and fold it in half to mark its exact midpoint.
  • Fasten loops to pegs at $X$ and $Y$. Stretch the rope tight by pulling the midpoint upwards to point $A$.
  • Similarly, pull the midpoint downwards until both halves are taut to point $B$.
  • Line $AB$ is the perpendicular bisector! (Since $AX = AY$ and $BX = BY$).

Angle Bisection

Angle Bisection & Creating $45^\circ$ Angles

Slide 14 / 28
✂️ Steps for Bisecting $\angle XOY$
  • With vertex $O$ as centre, draw an arc intersecting arms at $A$ and $B$, making $OA = OB$.
  • With centres $A$ and $B$ and equal radius, draw arcs intersecting at point $C$.
  • Draw ray $OC$. Then $OC$ bisects $\angle AOB$.
$$\triangle OBC \cong \triangle OAC \text{ (SSS)} \implies \angle BOC = \angle AOC$$
🌸 Applications & Halving
  • Constructing $45^\circ$: Construct a $90^\circ$ angle first, then bisect it: $$\frac{90^\circ}{2} = 45^\circ, \quad \frac{45^\circ}{2} = 22.5^\circ$$
  • 8-Petalled Flower Design: Divide the full $360^\circ$ circle into 8 equal sectors of $45^\circ$ each by successively bisecting straight and right angles.
  • Petal arcs are then drawn using radial centres on each ray.

Copying Angles

Copying a Given Angle (SSS Method)

Slide 16 / 28
🔄 Steps to Copy $\angle A$ to Ray $XZ$
  • Draw an arc of radius $r$ centered at vertex $A$, intersecting the arms at points $B$ and $C$.
  • Draw an arc of the same radius $r$ centered at new vertex $X$, intersecting ray $X$ at point $Z$.
  • Measure distance $BC$ with the compass.
  • Place compass point at $Z$ and swing an arc of length $BC$ to cut the first arc at point $Y$.
  • Draw ray $XY$. Then $\angle YXZ = \angle CAB$.
🔍 Why It Works: SSS

By construction, we have built two triangles with identical corresponding side lengths:

$$AB = XY = r, \quad AC = XZ = r, \quad BC = YZ$$

Therefore, $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle XYZ$ by the SSS condition, guaranteeing corresponding angles $\angle A = \angle X$.

Parallel Lines

Constructing Parallel Lines

Slide 18 / 28
📏 Corresponding Angles Principle
  • If two lines are cut by a transversal such that a pair of corresponding angles are equal, then the two lines are parallel.
  • To draw a line parallel to $m$ through an external point $B$:
    1. Draw any transversal line $l$ passing through $B$ and cutting line $m$ at $A$.
    2. At point $B$, copy the angle formed at $A$ (angle $a$) on the same side of transversal $l$.
✨ Construction Steps
  • Draw arc from $A$ cutting line $m$ at $C$ and line $l$ at $D$.
  • With the same radius, draw an arc from point $B$ cutting line $l$ at $F$.
  • Set compass to distance $CD$. From $F$, cut the arc at point $E$.
  • Draw line $n$ through points $B$ and $E$.
$$n \parallel m \quad (\text{since corresponding } \angle a = \angle a)$$

Architectural Arches

Architectural Arches: Trefoil & Pointed

Slide 20 / 28
🏛️ Trefoil Arch (Diwan-i-Aam)
  • Supporting Skeleton: An isosceles trapezoid $ABCD$ where base angles are equal:
    $$\angle BAD = \angle CDA \quad \text{and} \quad AB = CD$$
  • Side arcs are drawn from centres on the supporting legs.
  • A central circular arc connects points $B$ and $C$ to form the majestic multi-lobed trefoil crown.
🕌 Pointed Arch & Wavy Waves
  • Seen throughout medieval Indian & Gothic architecture.
  • Built on two inclined line segments of equal length meeting at an apex peak.
  • Finding the midpoints of both segments allows arcs of equal radii to be drawn on either side, producing an inflection curve or smooth pointed cusp.

Regular Hexagon

Regular Hexagon & The $60^\circ$ Angle

Slide 22 / 28
📐 Why 6 Equilateral Triangles Fit
  • The total angle around any point in a plane is $360^\circ$.
  • Each angle of an equilateral triangle is $60^\circ$.
  • Since $6 \times 60^\circ = 360^\circ$, six congruent equilateral triangles fit perfectly around a central vertex without gaps or overlaps.
  • Each interior corner angle of the resulting hexagon is:
    $$60^\circ + 60^\circ = 120^\circ$$
🧭 Constructing $60^\circ$ and Hexagon
  • Draw an arc of radius $r$ centered at $A$ cutting ray at $B$.
  • With the same radius $r$, swing an arc from $B$ cutting the first arc at $C$. Since $AB = BC = CA = r$, $\triangle ABC$ is equilateral $\implies \angle CAB = 60^\circ$.
  • Angle Bisections: $$\frac{60^\circ}{2} = 30^\circ, \quad \frac{30^\circ}{2} = 15^\circ$$
  • Hexagon of side $s$: Draw circle of radius $s$. Step off chords equal to $s$ around the circumference!

Tangrams & Tiling

Tangrams & Rectangular Grid Tiling

Slide 24 / 28
🧩 The 7 Tangram Pieces
  • An ancient Chinese dissection puzzle consisting of 7 shapes cut from a single square: 5 triangles (2 large, 1 medium, 2 small), 1 square, and 1 parallelogram.
  • Rearranged into birds, fish, running figures, letters, and animals without overlapping.
🔲 Tiling $m \times n$ Grids
  • Both $m$ and $n$ Even: Always tileable! (Fill each column using vertical tiles since row count is even).
  • One Even, One Odd: Always tileable! Lay tiles along the even dimension.
  • Both $m$ and $n$ Odd: IMPOSSIBLE!
    $$\text{Total squares} = m \times n = \text{Odd}$$
    Each $2 \times 1$ tile covers 2 squares. An integer sum of 2s is always even, so an odd grid can never be covered!

The Power of Coloring

Invariant Proofs: The Chessboard

Slide 25 / 28
♟️ The Invariant Principle
  • Consider a $5 \times 3$ grid with 1 corner square removed: total squares = $15 - 1 = 14$ (an even number!).
  • Even though 14 is divisible by 2, is it always tileable with $2 \times 1$ dominoes?
  • Color the grid like a chessboard: Adjacent squares have opposite colors (black and white).
  • Crucial Invariant: Every single $2 \times 1$ tile, placed horizontally or vertically, MUST cover exactly 1 black and 1 white square.
⚖️ The Impossibility Proof
  • Any valid tiling with $k$ dominoes must cover exactly $k$ black and $k$ white squares.
  • Therefore, a necessary condition for tileability is:
    $$\text{Number of White} = \text{Number of Black}$$
  • In the $5 \times 3$ grid of Fig. 6.14, there are 8 white squares and only 6 black squares ($8 \neq 6$).
  • Conclusion: It is mathematically impossible to tile! Introducing color turns an elusive puzzle into a trivial count.

Summary

Chapter Summary & Core Takeaways

Slide 27 / 28
✅ Geometric Constructions
  • Bisection: Dividing an object into two identical parts.
  • Perpendicular Bisector Locus: Any point equidistant from endpoints $X$ and $Y$ lies on the perpendicular bisector ($AX = AY$).
  • $90^\circ$ at a Point: Extend line around $O$ to make $OX = OY$, then erect bisector.
  • Angle Bisection: SSS triangle congruence condition ($\triangle OBC \cong \triangle OAC$).
  • Angle Duplication: Transfer arc radius and chord length to duplicate angles ($SSS$).
  • Parallel Lines: Constructing equal corresponding angles along a transversal.
✅ Regular Polygons & Tilings
  • $60^\circ$ Angle: Equilateral triangle construction with compass. Repeated halving yields $30^\circ, 15^\circ$.
  • Regular Hexagon: Formed by 6 congruent equilateral triangles meeting at a central point ($6 \times 60^\circ = 360^\circ$).
  • Tiling: Covering a flat region with shapes without gaps or overlaps.
  • Grid Parity: An $m \times n$ grid is tileable by $2 \times 1$ dominoes if and only if $m \times n$ is even.
  • Coloring Invariant: Domino tiling requires equal counts of black and white squares.
  • Plane Tilings: Squares, equilateral triangles, hexagons, and Escher art.
