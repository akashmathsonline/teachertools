3.1 Recap

🥣 Proportionality — A Quick Recap

Page 55
📖 What is a Proportional Relationship?

When two or more related quantities change by the same multiplicative factor, we call that relationship a proportional relationship.

  • Idli Batter Recipe: For $2\text{ cups}$ of rice, we add $1\text{ cup}$ of urad dal ($2:1$).
  • Viswanath: Mixes $6\text{ cups}$ rice with $3\text{ cups}$ dal ($6:3$).
  • Puneet: Mixes $4\text{ cups}$ rice with $2\text{ cups}$ dal ($4:2$).
  • Both scale the original recipe by factor 3 and factor 2 respectively!
🔄 Cross-Multiplication Test

To test whether two ratios $a:b$ and $c:d$ form a true proportion:

$$a:b::c:d \iff a \times d = b \times c \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{a}{c} = \frac{b}{d}$$
For Viswanath ($6:3$) and Puneet ($4:2$): $$6 \times 2 = 12 \quad \text{and} \quad 3 \times 4 = 12$$ Products are identical, proving proportional equivalence!

3.2 Map Ratios

🗺️ Ratios in Maps: The Representative Fraction (RF)

Page 56
🧭 Understanding RF 1:60,00,000

A Representative Fraction (RF) expresses the direct ratio between a measured distance on a paper map and the corresponding real-world distance on the ground.

$$\text{Scale Ratio } = \frac{\text{Distance on Map}}{\text{Actual Geographical Distance}}$$
  • $RF = 1:60,00,000$ signifies that $1\text{ cm}$ on the map represents $60,00,000\text{ cm}$ on the ground.
  • Geographical Distance: Refers to the straight-line aerial distance ("as the crow flies"), NOT the winding road distance!

3.3 Multi-Term Ratios

🌶️ Multi-Term Ratios: The Spice Mix Recipe

Page 57–58
🥣 Viswanath's 4-Ingredient Mix

Viswanath experiments with a traditional spice powder recipe consisting of 4 parts:

$$\text{Coriander} : \text{Red Chillies} : \text{Toor Dal} : \text{Methi} = 8 : 4 : 2 : 1$$
  • Scaling with Multiple Terms: Ratios can contain 3, 4, or more terms.
  • To preserve identical flavor, every term must be scaled by the exact same factor.
  • If Puneet only has 2 red chillies (half of 4), all ingredients are multiplied by $\frac{1}{2}$: $$8:4:2:1 :: 4:2:1:0.5$$
📜 General Rule for Multi-Term Proportion

When two multi-term ratios are proportional:

$$a : b : c : d :: p : q : r : s \iff \frac{a}{p} = \frac{b}{q} = \frac{c}{r} = \frac{d}{s}$$

Every pairwise quotient between corresponding components remains strictly invariant!

3.3 Practical Applications

🎨 Paint Mixing & Structural Concrete Ratios

Page 58
🎨 Example 1: Purple Paint ($2:3:5$)

Ratio: $\text{Red} : \text{Blue} : \text{White} = 2 : 3 : 5$.
Yasmin has $10\text{ litres}$ of white paint.

$$5\text{ parts} = 10\text{ litres} \implies 1\text{ part} = 10 \div 5 = 2\text{ litres}$$ $$\text{Red} = 2 \times 2 = \mathbf{4\text{ litres}}$$ $$\text{Blue} = 3 \times 2 = \mathbf{6\text{ litres}}$$ $$\text{Total Volume} = 4 + 6 + 10 = \mathbf{20\text{ litres}}$$
🏗️ Example 2: High-Strength Concrete

Ratio: $\text{Cement} : \text{Sand} : \text{Gravel} = 1 : 1.5 : 3$.
Given: $3\text{ bags}$ of cement available.

Scaling factor $k = 3$: $$\text{Cement} = 1 \times 3 = \mathbf{3\text{ bags}}$$ $$\text{Sand} = 1.5 \times 3 = \mathbf{4.5\text{ bags}}$$ $$\text{Gravel} = 3 \times 3 = \mathbf{9\text{ bags}}$$ $$\text{Total Concrete} = 3 + 4.5 + 9 = \mathbf{16.5\text{ bags}}$$

3.4 Dividing a Whole

⚖️ Dividing a Given Total in a Multi-Term Ratio

Page 58–59
📖 The Mathematical Principle

When dividing a known total quantity $X$ into a multi-term ratio $a : b : c : \dots$:

$$\text{Share of Component } A = X \times \frac{a}{a + b + c + \dots}$$
  • Step 1: Compute sum of all terms: $S = a + b + c + \dots$
  • Step 2: Determine unitary value of 1 part: $k = \frac{X}{S}$.
  • Step 3: Multiply each term by $k$ to allocate exact quantities.
🏗️ Example 3: 110 Units of Concrete

Ratio: Cement : Sand : Gravel = $1 : 1.5 : 3$. Total needed = $110\text{ units}$.

$$\text{Sum of terms} = 1 + 1.5 + 3 = 5.5\text{ parts}$$ $$\text{Scale multiplier} = 110 \div 5.5 = \mathbf{20}$$ $$\text{Cement} = 1 \times 20 = \mathbf{20\text{ units}}$$ $$\text{Sand} = 1.5 \times 20 = \mathbf{30\text{ units}}$$ $$\text{Gravel} = 3 \times 20 = \mathbf{60\text{ units}}$$

3.4 Geometry Link

📐 Geometric Application: Triangles in Ratios

Page 60
🔺 Example 5: Angle Ratio $1:3:5$

The interior angles of any triangle sum to $180^\circ$. If angles are in ratio $1:3:5$:

$$\text{Sum of parts} = 1 + 3 + 5 = 9\text{ parts}$$ $$\angle A = 180^\circ \times \frac{1}{9} = \mathbf{20^\circ}$$ $$\angle B = 180^\circ \times \frac{3}{9} = \mathbf{60^\circ}$$ $$\angle C = 180^\circ \times \frac{5}{9} = \mathbf{100^\circ}$$

Verification: $20^\circ + 60^\circ + 100^\circ = 180^\circ$ (Obtuse scalene triangle).

⚠️ Sidelength Ratio $1:3:5$ Warning!

Can you construct a triangle with side lengths in ratio $1:3:5$?

$$\text{Triangle Inequality Theorem: } a + b > c$$
  • Here, shortest sides: $1 + 3 = 4$.
  • Longest side: $5$.
  • Since $4 < 5$, the two shorter segments cannot even touch!
  • Impossible triangle! Ratio constraints must respect geometric laws.

3.5 Construction

📐 Step-by-Step Pie Chart Drafting Method

Page 61–62
1. Simplify Ratios

Find $\gcd$ of all category frequencies. Divide each frequency by $\gcd$ to obtain lowest terms: $$12:10:8:6:4 \xrightarrow{\div 2} 6:5:4:3:2$$ Sum of simplified parts $= 20$.

2. Calculate Central Angles

Multiply each fraction by full circle angle ($360^\circ$): $$\text{Per Part} = \frac{360^\circ}{20} = 18^\circ$$ Grade $A = 6 \times 18^\circ = 108^\circ$
Grade $B = 5 \times 18^\circ = 90^\circ$

3. Draw Radius & Measure

Draw base radius $AB$. Use a protractor centered at $A$ to sweep each calculated angle anti-clockwise:
• $AB \to AC$ ($108^\circ$)
• $AC \to AD$ ($90^\circ$)
• Label and color distinct slices.

3.6 Variations

🚗 Direct vs Inverse Proportions

Page 63–64
🚙 The Travel Time Dilemma

Traveling between Lucknow and Kanpur ($90\text{ km}$ fixed ground distance):

ModeSpeed ($x$)Time ($y$)Product ($xy$)
Walk$5\text{ km/h}$$18\text{ h}$$90$
Bicycle$15\text{ km/h}$$6\text{ h}$$90$
Motorcycle$30\text{ km/h}$$3\text{ h}$$90$
Car$60\text{ km/h}$$1.5\text{ h}$$90$
⚖️ Defining Inverse Proportions

When speed triples ($\times 3$), time divides by 3 ($\times \frac{1}{3}$).

$$x \times y = k \quad (\text{Constant Product})$$
  • Direct: Quotient is constant: $\frac{x_1}{y_1} = \frac{x_2}{y_2}$.
  • Inverse: Product is constant: $x_1 y_1 = x_2 y_2 = k$.
  • Equivalent ratio form: $\frac{x_1}{x_2} = \frac{y_2}{y_1}$.

3.6 Worked Models

⚡ Workers, Pumps & Food Stock Inverses

Page 65–66
1. Road Workers

20 workers take 4 days to lay a road. How long will 10 workers take?

$$x_1 y_1 = x_2 y_2$$ $$20 \times 4 = 10 \times y_2$$ $$y_2 = \frac{80}{10} = \mathbf{8\text{ days}}$$ Half the workers $\implies$ double the days!
2. Water Pumps

2 pumps fill a tank in 18 hours. How long if 2 more pumps are added (total 4 pumps)?

$$x_1 y_1 = x_2 y_2$$ $$2 \times 18 = 4 \times x$$ $$x = \frac{36}{4} = \mathbf{9\text{ hours}}$$ Doubling pump count halves pumping duration.
3. Hostel Provisions

Provisions feed 80 students for 15 days. If 20 more students join (total 100), how long does food last?

$$80 \times 15 = 100 \times x$$ $$1200 = 100x \implies \mathbf{x = 12\text{ days}}$$ More students $\implies$ fewer days supply lasts.

3.6 Work & Time

⏱️ Combined Work Rates: Vegetable Chopping

Page 66–67
🥕 Example 6: Cutting Vegetables Together

Ram cuts a batch of vegetables in $1\text{ hour}$. Shyam cuts the same batch in $1.5\text{ hours}$ ($= \frac{3}{2}\text{ h}$). How long if they work together?

  • Let 1 whole batch $= 1\text{ unit of work}$.
  • Ram's 1-hour rate: $1 \div 1 = \mathbf{1\text{ unit/hour}}$.
  • Shyam's 1-hour rate: $1 \div 1.5 = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}\text{ unit/hour}}$.
  • Combined 1-hour work: $$1 + \frac{2}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{5}{3}\text{ units/hour}}$$
⏳ Direct Proportion Step

Work completed and time taken are directly proportional:

$$\text{Time to finish } 1\text{ unit} = \frac{1}{\text{Combined Rate}} = \frac{1}{5/3} = \mathbf{\frac{3}{5}\text{ hour}}$$
Convert to minutes: $$\frac{3}{5} \times 60\text{ minutes} = \mathbf{36\text{ minutes}}$$ Working collaboratively cuts total task duration significantly!

Commercial Math

🏭 Industrial Scaling: Factory Machines & Speed

Page 68
🧸 Factory Machines & Toy Deadlines

A factory needs 42 machines to complete a toy order in 63 days. How many machines are needed to finish in 54 days?

Machines ($m$) and days ($d$) vary inversely ($m \times d = k$): $$42 \times 63 = m_2 \times 54$$ $$m_2 = \frac{42 \times 63}{54} = \frac{7 \times 63}{9} = 7 \times 7 = \mathbf{49\text{ machines}}$$ $$\text{Additional machines required} = 49 - 42 = \mathbf{7\text{ extra machines}}$$
🚗 Highway Car Speed vs Travel Time

A car takes 2 hours traveling at $60\text{ km/h}$. How long will it take at $80\text{ km/h}$?

Total distance $= 60 \times 2 = 120\text{ km}$. $$\text{Time } t_2 = \frac{120\text{ km}}{80\text{ km/h}} = \mathbf{1.5\text{ hours (1 hour 30 min)}}$$ Check inverse equation: $60 \times 2 = 80 \times 1.5 = 120$ ✅

Chapter Summary

📋 Master Summary Checklist

Page 69
✅ Multi-Term & Direct Proportions
  • Multi-term Ratio: $a:b:c:d$ indicates consistent multiplicative scaling across all terms.
  • Dividing a Whole: Share of component $p$ from total $X$ equals $X \times \frac{p}{p+q+r+\dots}$.
  • Representative Fraction (RF): Map distance to real ground distance ratio ($RF = 1:60,00,000 \implies 1\text{ cm} = 60\text{ km}$).
  • Pie Charts: Total circular angle $360^\circ$ split proportionally by category shares: $\text{Angle} = \frac{\text{Value}}{\text{Total}} \times 360^\circ$.
✅ Inverse Variations & Rates
  • Direct Variation: Quotient remains constant: $\frac{x_1}{y_1} = \frac{x_2}{y_2} = k$.
  • Inverse Variation: Product remains constant: $x_1 y_1 = x_2 y_2 = k$. When one quantity scales by $n$, the other scales by $\frac{1}{n}$.
  • Speed & Time: Distance $= \text{Speed} \times \text{Time}$ (Speed and time vary inversely).
  • Workers & Days: Total Work $= \text{Workers} \times \text{Days}$.
  • Combined Work: Add individual rates ($\text{units/hour}$); total time is reciprocal of combined rate.
